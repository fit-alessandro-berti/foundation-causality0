\abstract{%
Reliable causal analysis requires two decisions: which estimator to use, and whether the requested causal quantity is justified by the supplied assumptions and the available data. We introduce the Causal World Foundation Model (CWFM), a query-conditioned hybrid system that combines a pretrained neural risk router with a library of classical causal estimators fitted to each submitted dataset. Before any estimation, deterministic gates check an explicit identification contract and empirical support conditions; queries that fail receive a structured refusal with a machine-readable reason. Uncertainty is calibrated with split conformal prediction on a separate synthetic stream. On a locked synthetic benchmark of 180 answerable queries spanning static, randomized, and network-interference settings, CWFM attains a pooled mean absolute error of 0.166, comparable to the best single fixed baseline at 0.167, with 90\% interval coverage of 0.906, and it refuses all 120 queries whose declared identification or support conditions fail. Correctly chosen per-scenario specialists remain better in individual cells, and accuracy degrades substantially on one interference family outside the training prior. Diagnostics show that all point estimates come from the compiled classical blend; the neural residual and world-particle modules are inactive. The results support reliability-gated estimator orchestration as a practical role for pretrained causal systems.}

\keyword{causal inference; foundation models; estimator selection; identification; abstention; conformal prediction; network interference}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Many of the most important questions in science, medicine, and industry are \emph{causal}: What happens to the outcome if we change the treatment, the price, the policy, or the process? Standard machine-learning models are built to answer a different question, namely what is likely to happen if nothing is changed. A model can predict outcomes very accurately and still be completely wrong about the consequences of an \emph{intervention}, because prediction exploits every correlation in the data, including correlations that would disappear the moment we intervene~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. Answering causal questions therefore requires dedicated methods, and those methods rest on \emph{assumptions} about how the data were generated.

This is where causal analysis becomes fragile in practice. Before seeing the answer, the analyst must choose a model class, an estimator, an adjustment strategy, and often more specialized ingredients such as how influence spreads through a network. Each choice is an assumption, and a wrong choice does not merely reduce accuracy; it can produce conclusions that are \emph{confidently wrong}. Two examples from our own benchmarks illustrate this. First, when a linear model is fitted to data generated by a single smooth nonlinear mechanism, it can ``discover'' a change in the causal mechanism that does not exist, while its test loss improves substantially; the better fit rewards the wrong structural conclusion. Second, when an unobserved common cause affects both treatment and outcome, a standard estimator can detect an effect in every run while its estimate is severely biased and its confidence interval never contains the truth. In both cases the analysis looks successful by the usual metrics and is wrong precisely where it matters.

The state of the art offers two partial answers, each with a gap. On one side, classical causal inference provides a rich theory of \emph{identification} (determining whether a causal question can be answered at all from the available data and assumptions), together with well-understood estimators~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif,hudgens2008interference}. But every new dataset requires the analyst to select and configure these tools by hand, and the examples above show how easily that selection goes wrong. On the other side, a recent line of work pretrains transformer models on large collections of synthetic causal problems, so that a new dataset can be analyzed without refitting the pretrained network~\cite{ma2025causalfm,robertson2025dopfn,thompson2026arrow,qiao2026cdfm}. These \emph{causal foundation models} remove much of the manual selection burden, and several engage seriously with identification assumptions and uncertainty. Their interfaces, however, typically return an estimate \emph{within} a causal setting that is assumed by the training prior or supplied by the practitioner; to our knowledge, none exposes a formal, query-level refusal as a first-class output. When the requested quantity is not justified by the supplied assumptions or the data, this design risks automating exactly the false confidence described above.

In this paper we propose the \textbf{Causal World Foundation Model (CWFM)}, a hybrid system designed to combine the strengths of both sides. CWFM contains a transformer pretrained on a large stream of synthetic causal problems. Given a new dataset together with a formally specified causal question, its pretrained parameters stay fixed, while the classical estimators it orchestrates are fitted to each submitted dataset; we use the term foundation model only in this narrow sense of one pretrained checkpoint reused across many datasets and query types. Two design choices distinguish CWFM from prior causal foundation models. First, instead of acting as one universal neural estimator, it learns to \emph{select and combine} a library of well-understood classical estimators, adding a learned correction only where it has not been found to \emph{do harm}. Second, part of the causal setting is externalized as an explicit \emph{contract}: a deterministic identification check over the declared assumptions and a data-support check decide, before any estimate is produced, whether the requested question satisfies the implemented conditions. When it does not, CWFM \emph{refuses} and states why, instead of returning a misleading number. Its uncertainty intervals are \emph{calibrated} with split conformal methods rather than taken directly from the network.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a structured causal-query and assumption-contract interface, with deterministic identification and empirical-support gates that produce explicit, machine-readable refusal reasons for the implemented query classes;
\item a pretrained risk router over cross-fitted classical causal estimators, with compatibility masking and an empirically constrained neural residual, so that one shared checkpoint orchestrates estimation across static, randomized, and network-interference episodes without updating its neural parameters;
\item a separated training, development, calibration, and evaluation protocol that measures pooled accuracy, contract compliance, episode-level calibration, structural false positives, active computation pathways, and behavior under a shift of the pretraining distribution.
\end{itemize}
As a reproducibility commitment, the model, the synthetic problem generators, and the complete experimental pipeline are openly available.

Our assessment is organized around five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1} (\emph{accuracy}): Can a single pretrained model match well-tuned task-specific estimators on causal questions that are answerable from the data?
\item \textbf{RQ2} (\emph{enforcement}): Does the runtime gate correctly enforce the declared identification and support requirements, without being bypassed by the learned components?
\item \textbf{RQ3} (\emph{structure}): Can the model detect genuine changes in a causal mechanism while rejecting spurious changes induced by model misspecification?
\item \textbf{RQ4} (\emph{generalization}): How does the model behave on problem families that were held out from its pretraining distribution?
\item \textbf{RQ5} (\emph{pathways}): Which computational pathways of the architecture actually produce the reported results?
\end{itemize}

The experimental results can be summarized as follows. On answerable questions, CWFM matches the best single fixed baseline in pooled error without knowing in advance which mechanism generated each dataset, although a correctly chosen per-setting specialist remains somewhat better in individual settings (RQ1). It refuses every query whose declared identification or support conditions fail, while the baselines return a number in all of them; this is enforcement of a declared contract, not detection of hidden confounding from the data (RQ2). It recovers most genuine mechanism changes and, unlike a standard splitting baseline, largely resists splitting a smooth nonlinear mechanism into fake regimes (RQ3). Accuracy transfers to most problem families excluded from pretraining but degrades sharply on the one family qualitatively unlike the training distribution, showing that reliable recognition of such mismatch is still missing (RQ4). Finally, the reported estimates are produced entirely by the classical estimator blend selected by the pretrained router; the free-form neural components are inactive at the evaluated checkpoint, which we report openly (RQ5).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on causal inference and causal foundation models. Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} introduces the necessary background concepts. Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents and motivates the CWFM architecture. Section~\ref{sec:assessment} describes the experimental design, answers the five research questions, and situates the pooled results among published causal foundation models. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Causal Inference and Causal Discovery}\label{sec:related-causal}

Structural causal models and the potential-outcomes framework give causal questions a precise meaning~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. They distinguish three tasks that look similar but are fundamentally different: predicting an outcome under the observed regime, estimating the effect of an intervention, and reasoning about what would have happened in a particular case under a different action. Their central tool is the theory of \emph{identification}: a set of rules, such as back-door adjustment, that determine whether a causal question can be answered from the available data under stated assumptions. When point identification fails, the theory does not stop; sensitivity analysis quantifies how conclusions change as hidden confounding grows~\cite{rosenbaum1983sensitivity}, and partial identification replaces a point estimate with an honest interval~\cite{manski1990bounds}. The framework also covers settings where units influence one another, such as treatments that spill over through a network~\cite{hudgens2008interference}.

A complementary line of research, \emph{causal discovery}, aims to learn the causal structure itself from data. Approaches include constraint-based algorithms~\cite{spirtes2000causation}, continuous optimization over graph structures~\cite{zheng2018notears}, invariance of causal mechanisms across environments~\cite{peters2016invariant}, discovery from heterogeneous and nonstationary data~\cite{huang2020heterogeneous}, and causal analysis of nonlinear time series~\cite{runge2019pcmci}. All of these methods are powerful, but they share a practical burden: for every new dataset, an expert must choose the algorithm, the model class, and the tuning parameters, and the true structure is not available to check the choice. This manual selection step is exactly where the failures described in the introduction arise, and it is the entry point for foundation models.

\subsection{Amortized Causal-Effect Estimation}\label{sec:related-cfm}

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) offer a way to remove the manual selection step. A transformer is pretrained on a large collection of synthetic problems whose ground truth is known by construction; afterwards, a new dataset is processed by the fixed network, which approximates Bayesian inference under the distribution of problems it was trained on~\cite{muller2022pfns}. This idea has produced strong general-purpose tabular models~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} and has been carried over to causal-effect estimation, the closest comparison group for this paper. CausalFM builds PFNs from priors over structural causal models and formalizes valid priors for back-door, front-door, and instrumental-variable settings~\cite{ma2025causalfm}. Do-PFN predicts interventional outcome distributions directly from observational data, without requiring the causal graph at inference time~\cite{robertson2025dopfn}. CausalPFN amortizes average and heterogeneous treatment-effect estimation for settings satisfying ignorability and reports Bayesian uncertainty~\cite{balazadeh2025causalpfn}. Extensions address temporal data with interventions~\cite{thumm2026causaltimeprior} and longitudinal treatment sequences~\cite{zare2026causallongpfn}. These systems engage seriously with identification: the causal setting is encoded in the training prior or supplied by the practitioner. What they do not expose is a runtime interface that checks whether a specific submitted query satisfies explicit identification and support conditions and refuses when it does not; their answers are produced within the assumed setting. Section~\ref{sec:assessment-comparison} contextualizes our pooled benchmark result against the published results of these systems.

\subsection{Amortized Causal Discovery}\label{sec:related-discovery}

A separate strand pretrains models to predict causal \emph{structure} rather than to estimate a specified effect. Arrow predicts a graph skeleton together with a variable ordering, which guarantees an acyclic result~\cite{thompson2026arrow}; TabCausal and CDFM predict causal edges across heterogeneous tabular problems~\cite{li2026tabcausal,qiao2026cdfm}; and partial background knowledge can be supplied to such models instead of being ignored~\cite{reuter2026partialgraphs}. These models are relevant to the structural components of our architecture, but they solve a different task from the effect-estimation benchmark studied here and are not direct competitors in it.

\subsection{Choosing among Causal Estimators}\label{sec:related-selection}

The component that actually produces CWFM's estimates, selection among candidate causal estimators, has its own literature. Because counterfactual ground truth is unavailable, model selection for causal estimators is harder than for predictors: counterfactual cross-validation constructs stable surrogate criteria for comparing candidate effect models~\cite{saito2020counterfactualcv}, empirical studies chart how well such criteria work for heterogeneous-effect estimation~\cite{mahajan2024empirical}, and selective machine learning chooses among candidate nuisance estimators for doubly robust functionals~\cite{cui2024selective}. CWFM differs from this line in four ways: its selector is pretrained across synthetic episodes rather than tuned per dataset; it receives a typed query and design metadata as input; it selects across several causal settings with one shared checkpoint; and it operates behind identification and support gates that can withhold the answer entirely.

\subsection{Reliability Layers for Causal Foundation Models}\label{sec:related-reliability}

Reliability mechanisms for amortized causal inference are developing quickly, and they occupy different layers that should not be conflated. Learned reliability heads attach diagnostic scores, for example for confounding or identifiability, to a pretrained temporal causal model; such scores are heuristics that reflect the training prior, since identifiability is a property of the causal model and its assumptions rather than a pattern certifiable from data~\cite{tcpfn2026reliability,ma2025causalfm}. Frequentist corrections adjust PFN estimates and uncertainty after the fact~\cite{ospc2026correction}. Conformal methods provide distribution-free interval guarantees~\cite{vovk2005conformal,lei2018distribution}, recently extended to causal estimates under graph uncertainty~\cite{causalguard2026}. Self-compatibility checks can falsify a causal analysis without ground truth by testing whether structures learned from variable subsets agree~\cite{faller2024selfcompatibility}, and selective prediction studies the trade-off between answering fewer questions and answering them more reliably~\cite{geifman2017selective}. CWFM's identification gate sits in yet another layer: it is deterministic logic, conditional on supplied assumptions, rather than a learned diagnostic or a statistical correction. These layers are complementary, and no single one of them replaces the others.

\subsection{Refusal, Sensitivity Analysis, and Partial Identification}\label{sec:related-partial}

Refusal is not the only defensible output when point identification fails. Depending on the setting, the appropriate answer can be an identified set~\cite{manski1990bounds}, a sensitivity curve indexed by an assumed confounding strength~\cite{rosenbaum1983sensitivity}, an estimate restricted to a common-support population, or a clearly labelled extrapolation under an additional model; amortized sensitivity analysis now makes some of these outputs cheap to produce~\cite{javurek2026sensitivitypfn}. The precise behavior of CWFM is therefore the following: it refuses to issue the \emph{requested point estimate under the supplied contract} when its implemented identification or support conditions fail. Producing partial-identification outputs instead of refusals is a natural extension, not a capability of the current system.

\subsection{Positioning and Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

In summary, amortized causal estimation, amortized discovery, causal estimator selection, and reliability mechanisms each exist, but in separate systems. The specific combination provided by CWFM is, to our knowledge, not available in any single system reviewed above: a structured causal query, a deterministic runtime assumption contract, an empirical support gate, a pretrained selector over classical estimators, and an explicit refusal status. Table~\ref{tab:positioning} makes this conjunction auditable. The claim is deliberately narrow: individual ingredients appear elsewhere, and the contribution of this paper is their integration behind one query interface, together with an evaluation protocol in which refusal behavior is measured as a first-class outcome.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

This section introduces the four concepts needed to follow the rest of the paper. Readers familiar with causal inference may skip to Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

\subsection{Structural Causal Models, Interventions, and Identification}\label{sec:prelim-scm}

A \emph{structural causal model} (SCM) describes a system as a set of variables, each generated by a mechanism from its direct causes plus independent noise~\cite{pearl2009causality}. As a running example, suppose a variable $X$ (say, the severity of a patient's condition) influences both a treatment decision $A$ and an outcome $Y$, and the treatment also influences the outcome. An \emph{intervention} replaces one mechanism by force: instead of letting doctors choose the treatment, we set it to a fixed value $a$, written $do(A=a)$. The canonical causal quantity in this paper is the \emph{average treatment effect} (ATE),
\begin{equation}
\tau \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid do(A=1)\right] \;-\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid do(A=0)\right],
\end{equation}
the expected change in the outcome if everyone were treated versus no one. The difficulty is that we only observe data from the system \emph{without} the intervention: sicker patients are treated more often, so simply comparing treated and untreated outcomes mixes the effect of the treatment with the effect of severity.

\emph{Identification} asks whether a causal quantity can, in principle, be computed from the observed data under stated assumptions. In the example, if all common causes of $A$ and $Y$ are observed (here, $X$), the ATE is identified by the \emph{back-door adjustment} formula: compare treated and untreated outcomes within each level of $X$, then average over the population. Estimating this formula by fitting a regression of $Y$ on $A$ and $X$ and averaging its predictions is called \emph{g-computation}; it is one of the classical estimators our model builds on. If, however, some common cause is unobserved (\emph{hidden confounding}), no amount of data from the same source recovers the ATE: the question is \emph{not identified}, and an honest analysis must say so, widen its answer to a range, or state which additional assumptions would be needed.

\subsection{Network Interference and Exposure Mappings}\label{sec:prelim-interference}

Classical causal inference assumes that treating one unit does not affect another. In many settings this is false: vaccinating my neighbors protects me, and a promotion in one store draws customers from nearby stores. This phenomenon is called \emph{interference}, and its analysis requires knowing which units are connected, typically given as a network over the units~\cite{hudgens2008interference}. Because a unit can be affected by many neighbors in many ways, one summarizes the treatments of its neighbors by an \emph{exposure mapping}, for example ``the fraction of my neighbors that are treated.'' A typical causal quantity is then a \emph{spillover effect}: the expected change in my outcome if my neighbors' exposure moved from a low to a high level while my own treatment stayed fixed.

Two practical conditions matter throughout this paper. First, the exposure mapping is an assumption; if influence actually depends on, say, edge weights or two-hop neighbors, an analysis based on the simple fraction can be biased. Second, the requested contrast must have \emph{overlap} (also called positivity): there must exist comparable units observed at both exposure levels. If nobody in the data ever had a highly treated neighborhood, no method can estimate what would happen in one, and the only correct answer is to refuse.

\subsection{Regimes and Mechanism Changes}\label{sec:prelim-regimes}

Real systems are often not governed by one mechanism. The relation between inputs and outcome may differ across subpopulations, for example below and above a threshold of some observed variable: a machine behaves differently past a temperature limit, a subsidy rule changes at an income cutoff. We call such mechanism differences \emph{regimes}, and \emph{regime determination} is the task of deciding whether the data were generated by one mechanism or several, and if several, which variable and threshold separate them.

The task hides a trap that plays a central role in this paper. A single smooth nonlinear mechanism can be approximated surprisingly well by two linear pieces. A procedure that fits linear models and searches for splits will therefore ``find'' a regime boundary in perfectly stationary nonlinear data, and its predictive accuracy will genuinely improve. The improvement is real; the structural conclusion is false. Deciding between ``one curved mechanism'' and ``two straight ones'' therefore requires more than predictive gain, which is why regime determination is treated as a structural question, separate from prediction, throughout this work.

\subsection{Amortized Inference and Conformal Calibration}\label{sec:prelim-amortized}

Traditionally, every new dataset requires selecting and fitting a new statistical model. \emph{Amortized inference} moves this cost to a single pretraining phase: a neural network is trained on a very large collection of synthetic problems whose true answers are known by construction, and it learns to map a dataset directly to an answer~\cite{muller2022pfns}. At deployment, the pretrained network is applied to a new dataset without updating its parameters. The strength of this approach is that the network has effectively seen millions of analyses; its weakness is that everything it knows comes from the distribution of synthetic problems it was trained on, so that distribution acts as a prior and is part of the model's assumptions. CWFM adopts the pretraining idea for \emph{selection}: its neural parameters stay fixed at deployment, but the classical estimators it chooses among are still fitted to each new dataset.

A learned network also outputs uncertainty, but a neural confidence value carries no guarantee. \emph{Split-conformal prediction} provides one~\cite{vovk2005conformal,lei2018distribution}. The idea takes three sentences: hold out a set of calibration problems the model has never used for training; record how wrong the model was on each; then widen the model's intervals on new problems until they cover the observed error distribution at the desired level, for example 90\%. Under mild conditions this yields intervals with a finite-sample validity guarantee, which is why, in this paper, all reported uncertainty comes from conformal calibration rather than from the network's raw confidence.

\section{Approach}\label{sec:approach}

CWFM takes a dataset together with a formal causal question and returns either a calibrated causal answer or an explicit, explained refusal. This section presents the architecture step by step. Every component exists to prevent one of the failures described in the introduction, and we state this motivation as each component is introduced.

\subsection{Design Principles and Overview}\label{sec:approach-overview}

Four principles shape the design.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Amortize the choice of method, not the method itself.} The model learns to select and combine well-understood classical estimators. It does not replace them with a free-form neural predictor.
\item \emph{A learned correction must earn its place.} A checkpoint is eligible only if its neural correction does not materially degrade the classical compiled estimate on designated development data; when in doubt, the classical estimate stands.
\item \emph{Decide whether the question is answerable before trying to answer it.} This decision is made by deterministic logic, not by the network.
\item \emph{Report only uncertainty that carries a guarantee, and refuse rather than guess.} Intervals come from conformal calibration; unanswerable questions receive a refusal, never a number.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows how these principles translate into a pipeline. The input is a \emph{causal episode} (the data plus everything known about it) together with a formal query that acts as a declared contract (Section~\ref{sec:approach-episode}). Before any estimation, two deterministic gates check whether the query satisfies the implemented identification and support conditions; if not, the pipeline exits with a structured refusal (Section~\ref{sec:approach-gates}). If the gates pass, a library of classical estimators is fitted to the episode, and a typed encoder with a pretrained risk router blends their candidate estimates into one answer (Sections~\ref{sec:approach-encoder} and \ref{sec:approach-estimation}). Conformal calibration then turns the answer into an interval with a stated coverage property (Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}). Two further components, structural world-particle scores and a gated neural residual, are implemented but presently exploratory (Sections~\ref{sec:approach-worlds} and \ref{sec:approach-estimation}). Section~\ref{sec:approach-data} describes the training and evaluation data, Section~\ref{sec:approach-training} the pretraining and the safety-constrained model selection, and Section~\ref{sec:approach-guarantees} states precisely what is and is not guaranteed.

\subsection{Causal Episodes and the Formal Query Language}\label{sec:approach-episode}

The input unit of CWFM is a \emph{causal episode}: the dataset together with everything relevant that is known about it. Concretely, an episode contains the observed values, indicators of which values are missing, a schema stating the type of each variable (continuous, binary, and so on), the role of each variable with respect to the question (treatment, outcome, or covariate), an optional relation matrix connecting the units when interference is possible, and declared design knowledge, for example that the treatment was randomized. Passing this context explicitly matters: whether an analysis is valid often depends less on the numbers than on how they were produced.

The question itself is a \emph{formal query}, a small structured object stating the claim type (associational or interventional), the treatment and outcome variables, the causal quantity requested (for example the ATE, or a spillover contrast between two exposure levels), and the target population. We insist on a formal query rather than a natural-language question for two reasons. First, small changes of wording must not silently change the quantity being estimated; the formal object makes the estimand explicit and fixed. Second, the claim type prevents a quiet upgrade of an associational finding into a causal one: an interventional answer can only be produced for a query that requested one and passed the gates. A language model may translate a scientist's question into this format, but the causal core operates on the structured object only.

Together, the query and the declared design knowledge form a \emph{contract}, and the whole system can be summarized as one function
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(E, Q) \;\rightarrow\; \bigl(S,\; \widehat{\tau},\; C_\alpha,\; R,\; D\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the episode, $Q$ the formal query, and $S$ a status taking one of four values: \texttt{answer}, \texttt{not identified}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{out of scope}. The point estimate $\widehat{\tau}$ and the calibrated interval $C_\alpha$ are present only when $S = \texttt{answer}$; $R$ is a machine-readable refusal reason, and $D$ contains diagnostics that inform the user but carry no guarantee. The three non-answer statuses are deliberately distinct: a query can be formally nonidentified under the declared assumptions, it can be identified in principle but outside the rule library implemented so far, or it can be identified but lack adequate finite-sample support. Table~\ref{tab:queryclasses} lists the implemented query classes with their conditions and compatible estimators.

\subsection{Typed Encoder and Mechanism Experts}\label{sec:approach-encoder}

The encoder converts an episode into internal representations. Each cell of the data table is embedded from its value, its missingness indicator, the type of its variable, and the role of its variable in the query. The encoding deliberately contains no embedding of the column position: the meaning of a variable should come from its role and type, not from being column four. We state this as a design intention rather than a property; as Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5} measures, the complete implementation is not yet exactly permutation equivariant.

Attention, the basic operation of transformer models by which each part of the input gathers information from related parts, then alternates along two axes. Attention across variables, within one sample, lets the model relate treatment, outcome, and covariates to one another. Attention across samples, within one variable, lets it see distributional patterns such as skewness, subgroup structure, or outliers. When the episode contains a relation matrix, an additional message-passing step lets each unit see summaries of its neighbors, which is what a spillover analysis needs.

The feed-forward layers are organized as a sparse \emph{mixture of mechanism experts}. Instead of one universal network, several small expert networks specialize in different mechanism families, and a learned gate activates only the most relevant ones for each episode. We call this the \emph{encoder expert gate}, to keep it clearly distinct from the estimator risk router of Section~\ref{sec:approach-estimation}: the former shapes internal representations, the latter chooses among fitted classical estimators. The encoder expert gate carries a simplicity prior: flexible experts are used only when the data demand them. The motivation comes directly from our benchmarks, where a simple linear method beats a flexible one when the truth is linear, and the flexible method wins when the truth has a threshold. A model with a single, uniformly flexible pathway tends to blur exactly this distinction.

\subsection{Structural Heads and Exploratory World Particles}\label{sec:approach-worlds}

Structural decoders read the encoder representations and output probabilities rather than hard decisions. The most important one in the current system is the \emph{regime decoder}. It is hierarchical and mirrors how an analyst would reason: first, is there evidence of a mechanism change at all; if so, which variable separates the regimes; at which threshold; and what kind of change it is. Because the stationary nonlinear trap of Section~\ref{sec:prelim-regimes} is the known failure mode here, the decoder is trained on matched pairs of episodes in which one curved mechanism and two straight ones look predictively similar but differ structurally, so that predictive gain alone never triggers a split.

The architecture also contains \emph{world particles}: a small set of scored hypotheses about the data-generating process, each combining a graph, a mechanism family, and a regime hypothesis. The design goal, which gives the model its name, is to separate uncertainty about \emph{which structure} generated the data from uncertainty about \emph{parameter values} within a structure. We present this module as implemented but exploratory, and we are deliberate about its current standing: no calibrated posterior objective is claimed for the particle scores, the final point estimate does not depend on them at the evaluated checkpoint, and Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5} shows that their scores remained uninformative in this run. They appear in this paper as an honest description of the architecture, not as part of the empirical contribution.

\subsection{Identification and Support Gates}\label{sec:approach-gates}

Before any estimate is produced, two gates decide whether the query should be answered at all.

The \emph{identification gate} checks whether the requested causal quantity is identified given the episode's declared assumptions and design knowledge, for example randomized assignment or the assumption that all relevant confounders are observed. This gate is deterministic logic, not a learned component, for a principled reason: identifiability is a property of the causal model and its assumptions, and it cannot be certified from data patterns alone. A learned score could only imitate the training prior and would produce exactly the confident wrong answers we aim to prevent. This gate is the direct response to the hidden-confounding failure of the introduction, where a conventional method detected an effect in every run while its estimate was severely biased.

The \emph{support gate} checks whether the requested contrast is present in the data: are there comparable units observed under both treatment values, or both exposure levels, for the population in the query? Two related notions must be kept apart here. \emph{Population positivity}, the assumption that the contrast occurs with positive probability in the population, is an identification assumption and belongs to the contract. \emph{Observed finite-sample support}, which the gate actually measures, is an empirical statistic of the submitted dataset compared against a threshold. The gate enforces the second: if too few comparable observations exist, any estimate would be extrapolation from the pretraining prior rather than evidence, and the output is suppressed.

When a gate fails, CWFM returns a \emph{structured refusal} whose reason code distinguishes the three cases introduced in Section~\ref{sec:approach-episode}: formally nonidentified under the declared assumptions, outside the implemented rule library, or inadequately supported in this sample. The refusal also names what would resolve it, for example an instrument, an experiment, or additional data in the unsupported region. Learned diagnostic heads accompany the gates with empirical signals such as estimated overlap and prior mismatch, but they can only add caution; they can never overrule a gate and force an answer.

\subsection{A Risk-Routed Library of Classical Estimators}\label{sec:approach-estimation}

When both gates pass, the answer is produced by classical statistics guided by the learned model, in three steps.

First, every member of a \emph{library of estimators} that is compatible with the query class (Table~\ref{tab:queryclasses}) is fitted to the episode's data, each producing its own candidate estimate; structurally incompatible estimators are masked out. Each member is a standard, well-understood method that is correct under a different mechanism class: linear g-computation, g-computation with interactions, a spline-based version, a piecewise exposure-response estimator for threshold spillovers, a design-based estimator for randomized episodes, and a null estimator that returns no effect. Internal regression fits use held-out folds, and held-out clusters in network episodes, so that no observation contributes to a prediction about itself. This step is genuine per-episode statistical fitting: what stays fixed at deployment are the neural parameters, not the estimators.

Second, the \emph{estimator risk router} predicts, from the episode representation, how large each estimator's error is likely to be on this particular episode, and blends the candidates accordingly:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{compiled}} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j(E, Q)\,\widehat{\tau}_j,
\qquad \sum_{j} w_j = 1,\quad w_j \geq 0,
\end{equation}
where the weights are obtained from the predicted risks of the compatible estimators. The router is supervised during pretraining: because every synthetic episode carries its true effect, the realized error of every estimator is observable there, and the router learns to predict it. We call $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{compiled}}$ the \emph{compiled estimate}. It is the answer classical statistics would give if an experienced analyst had chosen and blended the estimators; the router amortizes that choice.

Third, a neural \emph{residual} may adjust the compiled estimate. It is initialized at zero, passes through a learned gate, and is trained with a no-harm objective that penalizes any adjustment that worsens the compiled estimate far more than it rewards an improvement; checkpoint eligibility additionally requires that the residual does not materially degrade the compiled estimate on the development banks. If the residual cannot prove its worth during training, the gate stays closed and the final answer equals the compiled estimate. This is the architectural embodiment of principle 2: the burden of proof is on the neural correction, never on the classical estimate.

\subsection{Calibration}\label{sec:approach-calibration}

All reported uncertainty comes from split-conformal calibration (Section~\ref{sec:prelim-amortized}). A dedicated bank of calibration episodes, never used for training, records how wrong the model's answers are in practice; intervals on new episodes are then widened until they cover this error distribution at the target level. The conformal scores are normalized and stratified, so intervals adapt to episode difficulty: an episode routed to a risky estimator, or one where the estimators disagree, receives a wider interval. The same split-conformal protocol, on the same calibration bank, is applied to every baseline, so the coverage comparison in Section~\ref{sec:assessment} is like for like. The threshold at which the regime decoder declares a mechanism change is fitted on the same calibration bank rather than learned end to end, for the same reason: a decision with error-rate consequences should be set where its error rate can be measured.

The resulting property must be stated precisely. The guarantee is \emph{episode-marginal}: over future episodes exchangeable with the calibration stream, the interval contains the true effect with probability at least the nominal level. This is not a conventional confidence interval for one fixed data-generating process under repeated sampling, and it promises nothing for episodes outside the calibration distribution, such as the held-out families of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq4} or real data. Section~\ref{sec:approach-guarantees} places this alongside the other guarantees of the system.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Data}\label{sec:approach-data}

The data deserve particular attention, because for a pretrained causal model the training distribution is not an implementation detail: it is part of the model's assumptions. Everything CWFM knows, it knows from this distribution. We therefore describe it precisely.

All training and evaluation data are \emph{synthetic causal episodes} produced by a simulator, and this is a necessity rather than a convenience. Supervising a causal model requires labels that real data cannot provide: the true effect of an intervention, the true causal structure, and the true answer to the question ``should this query be refused?''. In a simulation these labels are known by construction. Each episode is created in three steps: first a complete causal world is sampled (a graph, mechanisms, an assignment process, and an effect size); then the world is run to produce an observed dataset; finally the ground-truth labels are recorded and hidden from the model, which must recover them from the data alone.

Episodes come from three task families, matching the implemented scope:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Static treatment effects.} Between 64 and 128 units are described by three to eight covariates; a binary treatment affects a continuous outcome. Scenarios include linear and nonlinear mechanisms with observed confounding, and randomized experiments.
\item \emph{Regime determination.} Datasets with five to nine covariates whose outcome mechanism either is stationary, splits at a threshold of one covariate (with strong or deliberately weak shifts), or is a stationary smooth nonlinear mechanism, the hard negative of Section~\ref{sec:prelim-regimes}. Exact nulls are always included.
\item \emph{Network interference.} Units are linked by a cluster-structured network and treated cluster by cluster with varying saturation; each unit's exposure is the treated fraction of its neighbors. Spillover mechanisms are linear, threshold-shaped, or exactly null.
\end{itemize}
Crucially, every family also generates \emph{unanswerable episodes} in which the correct output is a refusal: hidden confounding (an unobserved variable drives both treatment and outcome) and poor overlap or poor support (the requested contrast barely occurs in the data). These cases are mixed into training at substantial rates. A model never shown them would learn that producing an answer is always rewarded, which is precisely the behavior we must avoid.

To prevent the model from learning superficial shortcuts, the presentation of every training episode is randomized: rows are shuffled, columns are permuted while preserving their declared roles, covariate scales are varied, and a random subset of covariate values (up to five percent) is masked as missing. The model must therefore rely on the declared roles and types of variables, never on their position or scale.

Training and evaluation use five strictly separated streams, drawn from disjoint random-seed ranges so that no episode can appear in more than one stream. Table~\ref{tab:streams} summarizes them. Training episodes are generated online and never repeated: over 1,600 steps with 24 episodes per batch, the model sees 38,400 distinct episodes exactly once each. The final test bank is \emph{locked}: it is evaluated once, after all training, selection, and calibration decisions are frozen, and its 14 scenario cells with 30 seeds each are identical for CWFM and for every baseline.

The out-of-distribution development bank deserves a separate remark. It consists of entire mechanism and graph families that are excluded from training: hinge, multiplicative, and saturating outcome mechanisms; intercept, variance, and shifted regime changes; two random-network families unlike the cluster networks seen in training; and nonmonotone spillovers. Holding out whole families, rather than merely new random draws from familiar ones, is what allows Section~\ref{sec:assessment} to distinguish genuine generalization from memorization of the training prior.

\subsection{Pretraining and Safety-Constrained Model Selection}\label{sec:approach-training}

CWFM is pretrained on the online episode stream of Section~\ref{sec:approach-data}, and training proceeds in stages. The estimator library and the router are trained first, with the residual frozen, so that a sound compiled path exists before any neural correction is allowed. The gated residual is then trained under the no-harm objective, and finally the whole system is fine-tuned jointly at a low learning rate.

Model selection enforces the reliability requirements as \emph{eligibility constraints}, not as terms in a loss. A checkpoint is eligible only if, on the designated development banks, it does not materially degrade the compiled estimate, keeps the false-regime rate on stationary nonlinear episodes below a cap, and produces zero answers on queries that should be refused. Only the eligible checkpoints are then ranked, by their worst-group error on the held-out development families. A checkpoint with better average accuracy that violates any constraint is discarded. This ordering, reliability first and accuracy second, is deliberate: it prevents the selection process itself from trading reliability for benchmark scores. These are empirical constraints verified on specific development data, not universal guarantees; the next subsection makes this distinction explicit.

Finally, we state the implemented scope explicitly. The reference implementation covers static tabular effects, observed-regime determination, and network interference. Temporal mechanism changes, latent-variable structure, and identification with partially specified graphs are part of the design but not of the implementation evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:assessment}, and no claim in this paper depends on them.

\section{Assessment}\label{sec:assessment}

This section first describes the experimental design, then answers the five research questions of the introduction, one subsection each, adding after RQ1 a contextual comparison with published causal foundation models, and closes with qualitative case studies that complement the aggregate metrics with worked examples.

\subsection{Experimental Design}\label{sec:assessment-design}

All results below come from the locked final test bank of Section~\ref{sec:approach-data}, which is evaluated exactly once, after every training, selection, and calibration decision is frozen. The bank contains 14 scenario cells with 30 seeds each, identical for all methods. Six cells contain answerable effect queries (static linear, static nonlinear, and randomized treatment effects; linear, threshold, and null network spillovers), giving 180 queries in which the true effect is known and an estimate is expected. Four cells contain regime-determination problems (a strong split, a weak split, the stationary nonlinear hard negative, and a complete null). The remaining four cells are \emph{stress cells} with 120 queries that must be refused: hidden confounding and poor overlap in the static setting, and hidden confounding and poor support in the network setting.

CWFM is compared against five baselines that always answer: linear g-computation, g-computation with interactions, spline g-computation, a piecewise exposure-response estimator, and random forest g-computation. Each baseline is a strong specialist for at least one cell, so the comparison is deliberately unfavorable to a general model. The baseline set and CWFM's internal estimator library overlap but differ deliberately: random forest g-computation is a stand-alone baseline representing a generic flexible fit and is not one of CWFM's experts, while the design-based and null experts inside CWFM are not meaningful stand-alone baselines outside their specific cells. For regime determination, the baseline is model-based recursive partitioning~\cite{zeileis2008mob}, where a regime means a split of the conditional outcome mechanism at a threshold of one observed covariate. We also report \emph{CWFM (compiled only)}, which is the full system with the neural residual removed; the difference between the two isolates the contribution of the learned correction in RQ5.

One provenance clarification is needed for RQ4. The six held-out families evaluated there were excluded from all gradient training, but they also form the out-of-distribution development bank used to rank eligible checkpoints (Table~\ref{tab:streams}). They are therefore correctly described as excluded from gradient training, not as wholly unseen: checkpoint selection had access to aggregate performance on them. The implementation details are modest in scale: the encoder uses hidden dimension 96 with three attention blocks and four mechanism experts, the estimator library has six members, and the full training run takes about half an hour on one GPU; exact configurations, seeds, and package versions are recorded in the result manifest.

Metrics are the mean absolute error (MAE) of the estimated effect against the known truth, the empirical coverage and width of the 90\% intervals, the rate of false answers on the stress cells, and exact accuracy for regime determination (the detected structure must match the truth, including detecting nothing when there is nothing). Differences between methods are assessed with paired bootstrap confidence intervals over the shared seeds. All seeds, configuration values, and package versions are recorded in a result manifest together with a cryptographic hash of the evaluated checkpoint, so the experiment can be reproduced exactly.

\subsection{RQ1: Accuracy on Answerable Queries}\label{sec:assessment-rq1}

\emph{Answer: as a pooled generalist, yes. CWFM matches the best single fixed baseline in pooled error without knowing which setting it is in, and clearly outperforms the generic flexible baseline; a per-cell oracle of specialists remains ahead.}

\subsection{Contextual Comparison with Published Causal Foundation Models}\label{sec:assessment-comparison}

RQ1 compares CWFM against classical always-answer baselines on a shared locked benchmark. A natural further question is how the pooled result relates to the published causal foundation models reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related-cfm}. For this second comparison no common test set exists: CausalPFN, Do-PFN, and CausalFM each evaluate on their own benchmark distributions, with their own outcome scalings, estimands, and aggregation protocols. We therefore contextualize our result against their published numbers, without rerunning their implementations, and mark every entry of Table~\ref{tab:fm-comparison} with its evidence status: exact values (E) read directly from a repository or the comparator paper, derived values (D) obtained from a published score by a deterministic conversion, and indicative ranges (I) that summarize the order of magnitude of several benchmark-specific published results.

Two conversions are used. Where a comparator reports an RMSE-type error $r$, we report the point approximation $\sqrt{2/\pi}\,r \approx 0.798\,r$, which is exact for zero-mean Gaussian errors, together with a sensitivity band $[0.70\,r,\, 0.90\,r]$ covering the MAE-to-RMSE ratios of light- to moderately heavy-tailed error distributions. For CausalFM, whose principal published score is a PEHE of $0.515$, we additionally use the fact that PEHE is the root mean squared error over individual treatment effects: for effect errors $e_i$, $|\overline{e}\,| \le \sqrt{\overline{e^{2}}} = \mathrm{PEHE}$, so the published value yields only the loose same-sample bound of $0.515$ on the absolute ATE error and, under the Gaussian approximation, an ITE-level MAE proxy of $0.361$--$0.464$. In particular, the numerical inequality $0.166 < 0.515$ is \emph{not} a superiority claim: it compares a population-level effect MAE with an individual-effect RMSE on different test distributions and outcome scalings, and taking it at face value would reward the easier estimand.

Read with these caveats, Table~\ref{tab:fm-comparison} supports one deliberately modest conclusion. The closest published systems, CausalPFN and Do-PFN, occupy an indicative low-to-mid $10^{-1}$ ATE-MAE range on their own benchmarks, which overlaps our pooled MAE of 0.166 on ours. CWFM is therefore competitive in order of magnitude with published foundation models for causal-effect estimation, on its native evaluation and under the substantially stricter interface of Section~\ref{sec:related-gap}; the comparison does not establish a ranking in either direction. The amortized discovery models are excluded from the error comparison entirely, because structure-recovery metrics such as SHD, edge F1, or AUROC admit no valid conversion to effect error. Table~\ref{tab:fm-capabilities} instead records which causal targets each system principally evaluates, complementing the interface-level comparison of Table~\ref{tab:positioning}.

\subsection{RQ2: Enforcement of the Declared Contract}\label{sec:assessment-rq2}

\emph{Answer: yes. CWFM answered all 180 queries whose declared contracts were valid and refused all 120 queries whose declared identification or support conditions fail, without the learned components ever bypassing the gates. Every baseline returned a number in every stress cell.}

Before interpreting this result, one fact must be stated first: the identification status of each stress episode is supplied through its declared design and assumption metadata. CWFM does not infer the existence of an unobserved confounder from the observed data; no method can do so from the same observational distribution alone. What RQ2 tests is whether the runtime gate correctly enforces the declared conditions and whether the surrounding learned machinery ever circumvents it.

The stress cells are the queries where a number is a mistake under the declared contract: declared hidden confounding makes the effect unidentifiable, and poor overlap or support means the requested contrast barely occurs in the data. The always-answer baselines produce estimates in all of them, and those estimates inherit exactly the failure described in the introduction: they can look precise while being systematically wrong. CWFM instead returned a structured refusal naming the failed condition in every case, so the architecture prevented an estimate whenever the declared contract failed one of the implemented gate conditions. Two honest qualifications bound the claim. First, false metadata, an incomplete gate rule, or a mechanism outside the prior can still lead to a wrong answer; the gates enforce the contract, they do not verify it. Second, the same gating layer could in principle wrap any estimator; the comparison with ungated baselines demonstrates the value of the wrapper, not a unique property of CWFM's point estimator.

\subsection{RQ3: Regime Detection without False Splits}\label{sec:assessment-rq3}

\emph{Answer: largely yes. CWFM recovers most strong regime splits while rejecting most spurious ones, where the standard splitting baseline is fooled every time; weak splits remain out of reach for both.}

Table~\ref{tab:regime} compares CWFM with the model-based recursive partitioning baseline~\cite{zeileis2008mob}; the target structure is a split of the conditional outcome mechanism at a threshold of one observed covariate, and exact accuracy requires recovering the split variable and threshold, or the absence of any split, correctly. On strong splits the baseline is more sensitive (exact accuracy 0.800 versus 0.633). The decisive difference is the stationary nonlinear hard negative: the baseline declares a split in 100\% of these episodes, mistaking one curved mechanism for two regimes in every single run, while CWFM declares a split in only 16.7\% and identifies the correct stationary structure in 83.3\%. Both methods are essentially perfect on the complete null, and both fail on the deliberately weak splits, whose signal is simply too small at this sample size. Pooled over all four cells, CWFM reaches 0.617 exact accuracy versus 0.458 for the baseline. The trade of some sensitivity for a large gain in structural specificity is intentional and follows from the calibrated decision threshold of Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}.

\subsection{RQ4: Generalization to Held-Out Families}\label{sec:assessment-rq4}

\emph{Answer: mixed, and we report it as measured. Accuracy transfers well to most held-out families but fails on the one family that is qualitatively unlike anything in training, and the system did not recognize the failure.}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq4} shows effect MAE on the six mechanism and graph families excluded from gradient training; as noted in Section~\ref{sec:assessment-design}, these families also informed checkpoint selection, so they are excluded from training rather than wholly unseen. On hinge outcomes, multiplicative outcomes, and the unfamiliar Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi random graphs, error stays essentially at the in-distribution level. Saturating outcomes and small-world graphs roughly double it, which is a moderate and arguably acceptable degradation. Nonmonotone interference, however, fails badly, with an MAE of 1.357, roughly eight times the in-distribution level: spillovers that first rise and then fall resemble nothing in the training distribution, and the model has no basis for them.

\subsection{RQ5: Which Pathways Are Active?}\label{sec:assessment-rq5}

\emph{Answer: the point predictions are generated entirely by the compiled classical estimator blend. The neural residual and the world-particle module make no measurable contribution at the evaluated checkpoint, and we report this openly.}

The cleanest evidence is the comparison with CWFM (compiled only): the two systems produce point estimates that are equal to numerical precision. The gated neural residual remains almost closed (mean gate activation 0.0067) and its mean absolute correction is below $3\times10^{-8}$, so the no-harm training of Section~\ref{sec:approach-estimation} did exactly what it was designed to do, and no more: the residual never damaged the compiled estimate on the evaluated data, and it also never improved it. Similarly, the world-particle scores remained effectively uniform in this run, so that module, while architecturally in place, contributed nothing measurable. Note the limit of this analysis: it identifies which pathways are active, but it does not isolate the individual contributions of the estimator library, the router, and the gating logic from one another, since the benchmark does not ablate them separately.

Routing works but is not sharp. All six estimator experts stay in use, yet in threshold spillover episodes the estimator risk router assigns the piecewise expert only about 15\% of the weight although that expert is clearly the best there (Table~\ref{tab:scenario}: 0.173 versus 0.283 for CWFM's blend). Sharper episode-level risk prediction is the most direct path to improvement, and Table~\ref{tab:scenario} bounds the available headroom.

Finally, Table~\ref{tab:probes} reports invariance probes: the estimate should not change when the data are presented differently. Estimates are compared in the original outcome units, and each reported value is the average absolute change over a fixed bank of probe episodes; the full protocol is in the result manifest. Row shuffling and affine rescaling of the outcome change the estimate only at numerical noise level. Inserting an irrelevant covariate shifts it moderately, and reordering columns (with roles preserved) shifts it most: the permutation intention stated in Section~\ref{sec:approach-encoder} is not yet achieved, despite the randomized training presentation. Together with the diffuse routing and the inactive exploratory modules, these probes give the next revision of the system a concrete and measurable agenda.

\subsection{Qualitative Case Studies}\label{sec:assessment-qualitative}

Aggregate metrics say how often a method succeeds; they do not show \emph{what success and failure look like} on an individual dataset. This subsection therefore complements the quantitative results with worked examples produced by a small companion package included in our repository. The examples are drawn from the persisted datasets of the project's classical benchmark suite, from which the CWFM training episodes derive. Their selection is deterministic and honest: each success example uses the seed whose primary metric is closest to the median over runs, models are fitted only on the discovery sample, the generator truth is revealed only afterwards for annotation, and one case is a deliberately included failure. Two remarks frame the reading. First, these cases run the classical benchmark pipelines, so they characterize the problem landscape rather than CWFM itself. Second, three of the cases concern temporal and latent-variable structure, which is outside CWFM's implemented scope; we include them because they specify, at the level of concrete mechanisms, what the planned extensions must achieve.

\subsubsection{Regime Recovery in the Clean Case}
In the representative observed-regime example, the true mechanism splits at $X_5 \leq 0.000$ and the fitted tree selects the correct variable with threshold $0.027$. Recovery is not only structural but quantitative: Table~\ref{tab:qualregime} compares, for all twelve predictor-outcome relationships, the true change of the local coefficient across the regime boundary with the estimated change. Matched probes that hold ordinary predictors fixed and cross only the regime threshold confirm that the fitted local means track the true conditional means on both sides. This is what a genuinely recoverable regime looks like, and it is the behavior the strong-split cell of RQ3 summarizes in aggregate.

\subsubsection{Where Latent-Variable Errors Originate}
The final case, also outside the implemented scope, dissects error propagation in a latent-variable graph problem. The true structure is a chain over six latent variables. Figure~\ref{fig:qualgraph} compares three reconstruction levels that differ only in how much is estimated: Level A uses the true latent scores, Level B reconstructs the scores from noisy indicators with cross-loadings, and Level C additionally estimates the grouping of indicators. Level A recovers the chain exactly. Levels B and C recover every true edge but add the same two spurious edges, $Z_1$-$Z_5$ and $Z_2$-$Z_6$, whose estimated partial correlations ($-0.14$ and $0.09$) are absent in the truth. Because the three levels share everything except the measurement steps, the figure localizes the error: it originates in score reconstruction under cross-loadings, not in graph estimation. A related latent-group case shows the upstream step working well at moderate difficulty: the number of outcome-relevant groups is selected correctly (three of three), with 20 indicators correctly assigned, 20 irrelevant features correctly omitted, 3 relevant indicators missed, and 1 misassigned. Together these cases justify the design decision, stated in Section~\ref{sec:approach-worlds} as future work, that latent grouping should be propagated softly rather than fixed by a hard upstream decision.

\subsubsection{What the Case Studies Establish}
For the implemented scope, the regime and misspecification cases corroborate the RQ3 findings at the level of individual mechanisms: real regimes are recoverable with quantitatively faithful coefficient changes, and false regimes arise from misspecification with no predictive warning sign, which is exactly the failure the calibrated regime decoder is built to resist. For the planned extensions, the cases turn abstract requirements into concrete specifications: a temporal component must classify change types under calibrated error control, because false attributions can arrive at borderline significance with tiny estimated effects; and a latent-variable component must carry grouping uncertainty forward, because the observed graph errors demonstrably originate in measurement and grouping rather than in graph estimation.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Results Mean}\label{sec:discussion-meaning}

The results support a specific, and perhaps sobering, reading of what a pretrained system for causal inference should be. The measured performance did not come from a neural network that estimates effects better than classical statistics. It came from the compiled classical pathway: a library of classical estimators applied correctly, blended by a pretrained router, behind deterministic contract logic. The benchmark does not separate the individual contributions of these ingredients, but it establishes that the pathway producing every reported estimate is classical at its core. In other words, the foundation-model machinery earned its place by \emph{selecting and guarding} classical inference, not by replacing it.

We believe this is the realistic near-term value of the approach. Matching a correctly chosen specialist is of limited interest when the specialist is known; the practical problem is that in real analyses nobody tells the analyst which specialist is correct, and Table~\ref{tab:scenario} shows how costly a wrong choice is. A single system that is close to the winner in most cells, refuses the queries whose declared conditions fail, and reports calibrated intervals removes exactly the decisions that make applied causal analysis fragile. The zero false-answer rate deserves emphasis for a second reason: it was achieved by deterministic enforcement of a declared contract and verified by experiment, an ordering we consider healthier than hoping that a learned system happens to behave safely.

The safety-first selection protocol also deserves comment. The evaluated checkpoint was not the most accurate candidate seen during training; candidates with better average scores were discarded because they violated a safety constraint. Accepting this trade openly, and encoding it in the selection procedure rather than in the narrative, is in our view a practice worth adopting for learned causal systems in general.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

The study has clear limitations, and the assessment was designed to expose rather than obscure them. First, the free-form neural components are not yet pulling their weight: the gated residual never improved the compiled estimate, and the posterior over causal worlds remained uniform, so two of the architecturally central components currently contribute safety but no accuracy. Second, routing is diffuse; the router keeps all experts active instead of committing to the best one, leaving measurable headroom unused in the threshold cell. Third, variable-order equivariance is imperfect: reordering columns, which should not matter, shifts the estimate noticeably. Fourth, generalization collapses on the one excluded family that is qualitatively unlike the training distribution, the system did not recognize the failure, and those families also informed checkpoint selection, so even these numbers are not from wholly unseen data. Fifth, the implemented scope is the first slice of the design: static tabular effects, observed regimes, and network interference, without temporal changes, latent variables, or identification with partially specified graphs. Sixth, and most importantly, all evidence in this paper is synthetic. The locked test bank is generated by the same kind of simulator as the training data, so the reported accuracy is an upper bound on what should be expected on real data, where the prior is never exactly right. For the same reason, the contextual comparison of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-comparison} rests on published numbers obtained on different benchmarks, estimands, and scalings, and therefore supports only an order-of-magnitude competitiveness claim, not a ranking among causal foundation models.

\subsection{What a Causal Foundation Model Cannot Solve}\label{sec:discussion-cannot}

Some limits are not engineering debts but mathematical facts, and no amount of pretraining removes them. Hidden confounding cannot be overcome from the same observational data without additional assumptions, instruments, or experiments. No model can estimate a contrast in a region where no comparable data exist. And when several causal structures explain the observed data equally well, the direction of an edge inside that equivalence class is not recoverable from the data alone. A foundation model does not eliminate causal assumptions; it \emph{moves} them into the pretraining distribution. This relocation has a real benefit, because one explicit, versioned, and stress-tested prior replaces thousands of implicit per-analysis choices, but it makes the prior part of the scientific claim. It should be published, criticized, and revised like any other assumption.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:discussion-future}

The findings define a concrete agenda. On the current scope, the priorities are sharper episode-level risk prediction for the router, world particles that actually specialize, and full variable-order equivariance; RQ5 gives each of these a measurable target. On scope, the next extensions are temporal mechanism changes, latent-variable structure, and identification with partially specified graphs, together with sensitivity bounds so that near-violations of assumptions yield widened intervals rather than refusals. The qualitative case studies of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-qualitative} make these extensions concrete: the temporal component must classify change types under calibrated error control, since a false attribution arose at borderline significance with negligible estimated effects, and the latent component must propagate grouping uncertainty, since the observed graph errors originated in measurement and grouping rather than in graph estimation. On evidence, the essential step is leaving the synthetic world: evaluation on semi-synthetic benchmarks built from real covariate distributions, and on real datasets with randomized or quasi-experimental ground truth. Rich operational event data, for example from business processes and inventory systems, offer an attractive application domain, because they provide the temporal, relational, and interventional structure that the causal episode format is designed to carry.

%=================================================================
\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced CWFM, a reliability-gated system for causal inference that treats reliability as a design requirement rather than a hoped-for property. Given a dataset and a formally specified causal question, CWFM fits and combines classical estimators through a pretrained risk router, checks a declared identification contract and empirical data support with deterministic gates before producing any estimate, reports only conformally calibrated uncertainty, and refuses, with a stated reason, the queries whose conditions fail. On a locked synthetic benchmark, this single pretrained checkpoint matched the pooled accuracy of the best fixed baseline without knowing which mechanism generated each dataset, although a per-cell oracle of specialists stayed ahead; it kept its intervals near nominal episode-level coverage; and it answered none of the 120 queries whose declared identification or support conditions failed, while every baseline answered all of them. The analysis of the active pathways is equally clear: every reported estimate is produced by the compiled classical estimator blend, while the free-form neural components are inactive, their contribution an open opportunity rather than a demonstrated fact.

We close with the test that we believe should define success for this line of research. A causal foundation model that detects an effect under hidden confounding in every run, while its estimate is badly biased and its intervals never contain the truth, has not advanced causal inference; it has automated false confidence. The measure of progress is therefore not only how accurately a model answers, but whether it knows what it cannot know: whether it separates identified from unidentified questions, supported from unsupported contrasts, and calibrated confidence from mere fluency. CWFM is designed, trained, and selected to refuse whenever its declared contract fails, and the experiments show that this behavior can be obtained without giving up pooled accuracy on the questions that can be answered; recognizing the failures the contract does not declare, above all data from outside the training prior, is the clearly identified next step. We hope this standard, answer well and refuse well, becomes the default acceptance test for systems that claim to reason about cause and effect.

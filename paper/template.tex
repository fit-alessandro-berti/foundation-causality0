\abstract{%
Many practical questions are causal: they ask what would happen to an outcome if we changed a treatment, a price, or a policy. Answering such questions from data requires choosing an estimation method and, before that, deciding whether the question can be answered at all from the available data and assumptions. Both decisions are error prone, and wrong choices can produce answers that look precise while being wrong. We introduce the Causal World Foundation Model (CWFM), a hybrid system in which a neural network, pretrained on a large collection of synthetic causal problems, orchestrates a library of classical causal estimators that are fitted anew to each submitted dataset. Before any estimation, deterministic gates check a declared contract of identification and support conditions. Queries that fail these checks receive a structured refusal with a machine-readable reason instead of a number, and the uncertainty of every answer is calibrated with split conformal prediction. On a locked synthetic benchmark spanning static, randomized, and network-interference settings, CWFM matches the accuracy of the best fixed baseline without knowing which mechanism generated each dataset, keeps its intervals close to the nominal coverage, and refuses every query whose declared conditions fail while the baselines answer all of them. A confirmatory round under a frozen protocol, including a semi-synthetic benchmark built on real energy-sensor covariates, reproduces these findings and sharpens them: all point estimates come from the classical estimator blend, and simple non-neural aggregation baselines match or slightly exceed the pretrained router's accuracy. The demonstrated value of the system therefore lies in the contract, gating, and calibration layer around classical estimation, a practical and transferable role for pretrained causal systems.}

\keyword{causal inference; trustworthy artificial intelligence; interpretable artificial intelligence; foundation models; estimator selection; identification; abstention; conformal prediction; network interference}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Many of the most important questions in science, medicine, and industry are \emph{causal}: they ask what would happen to an outcome if we actively changed something, such as a treatment, a price, a policy, or a process step. Causality is not the same as prediction. A standard machine-learning model answers a different question, namely what is likely to happen if nothing is changed, and it can predict very accurately while being completely wrong about the consequences of an \emph{intervention}, because prediction exploits every correlation in the data, including correlations that would disappear the moment we intervene~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. A simple example makes the difference concrete. In observational medical data, patients who received an intensive treatment often show worse outcomes than patients who did not, simply because sicker patients are treated more intensively. The data faithfully report this association, yet the treatment may still help every patient who receives it. Predicting outcomes from such data is easy; deciding what would happen if more patients were treated requires separating the effect of the treatment from the effect of being sick in the first place. Answering causal questions therefore requires dedicated methods, and those methods rest on \emph{assumptions} about how the data were generated.

In practice, causal analysis faces two recurring problems. The first is \emph{method choice}. Before seeing the answer, the analyst must choose a model class, an estimator, an adjustment strategy, and often more specialized ingredients such as how influence spreads through a network. Each choice is an assumption, and a wrong choice does not merely reduce accuracy; it can produce conclusions that are \emph{confidently wrong}. Two examples from our own benchmarks illustrate this. When a linear model is fitted to data generated by a single smooth nonlinear mechanism, it can ``discover'' a change in the causal mechanism that does not exist, while its test loss genuinely improves. And when an unobserved common cause affects both treatment and outcome, a standard estimator can detect an effect in every run while its estimate is severely biased and its confidence interval never contains the truth. In both cases the analysis looks successful by the usual metrics and is wrong precisely where it matters.

The second problem is \emph{answerability}. Some causal questions cannot be answered from the available data at all: when an unobserved factor drives both the treatment and the outcome, or when the data contain no units comparable to those the question asks about, no method can recover the answer. An honest analysis must recognize such questions and decline them, yet most analysis pipelines and most learned models always return a number. The state of the art offers two partial answers, each with a gap. On one side, classical causal inference provides a rich theory of \emph{identification} (determining whether a causal question can be answered at all from the available data and assumptions), together with well-understood estimators~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif,hudgens2008interference}. But every new dataset requires the analyst to select and configure these tools by hand, and the examples above show how easily that selection goes wrong. On the other side, a recent line of work pretrains transformer models on large collections of synthetic causal problems, so that a new dataset can be analyzed without refitting the pretrained network~\cite{ma2025causalfm,robertson2025dopfn,thompson2026arrow,qiao2026cdfm}. These \emph{causal foundation models} remove much of the manual selection burden, and several engage seriously with identification assumptions and uncertainty. Their interfaces, however, typically return an estimate \emph{within} a causal setting that is assumed by the training prior or supplied by the practitioner; to our knowledge, none exposes a formal, query-level refusal as a first-class output. When the requested quantity is not justified by the supplied assumptions or the data, this design risks automating exactly the false confidence described above. Figure~\ref{fig:bridge} summarizes the two incomplete halves and the combination proposed in this paper.

In this paper we propose the \textbf{Causal World Foundation Model (CWFM)}, a hybrid system designed to combine the strengths of both sides and to address the two problems above directly. CWFM contains a transformer pretrained on a large stream of synthetic causal problems. Given a new dataset together with a formally specified causal question, its pretrained parameters stay fixed, while the classical estimators it orchestrates are fitted to each submitted dataset; we use the term foundation model only in this narrow sense of one pretrained checkpoint reused across many datasets and query types. Two design choices distinguish CWFM from prior causal foundation models, one per problem. For method choice, instead of acting as one universal neural estimator, CWFM learns to \emph{select and combine} a library of well-understood classical estimators, adding a learned correction only where it has not been found to do harm. For answerability, part of the causal setting is externalized as an explicit \emph{contract}: deterministic identification and data-support checks decide, before any estimate is produced, whether the requested question satisfies the implemented conditions. When it does not, CWFM \emph{refuses} and states why, instead of returning a misleading number; its uncertainty intervals are calibrated with split conformal methods rather than taken directly from the network.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a structured causal-query and assumption-contract interface, with deterministic identification and support gates that produce explicit, machine-readable refusal reasons for the implemented query classes;
\item a pretrained risk router over a library of classical causal estimators, including a cross-fitted doubly robust (AIPW) pathway, with compatibility masking and a gated neural residual, so that one shared checkpoint orchestrates estimation across static, randomized, and network-interference episodes without updating its neural parameters;
\item a strictly separated training, calibration, and evaluation protocol measuring accuracy, contract compliance, calibration, structural false positives, and behavior under distribution shift, followed by a protocol-frozen confirmatory round with non-neural aggregation baselines, a gate-compliance property test, a frozen invariance bank, and a semi-synthetic benchmark on real sensor covariates.
\end{itemize}
As a reproducibility commitment, the model, the synthetic problem generators, and the complete experimental pipeline are openly available.

Our assessment is organized around five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1} (\emph{accuracy}): Can a single pretrained model match well-tuned task-specific estimators on causal questions that are answerable from the data?
\item \textbf{RQ2} (\emph{enforcement}): Does the runtime gate correctly enforce the declared identification and support requirements, without being bypassed by the learned components?
\item \textbf{RQ3} (\emph{structure}): Can the system's calibrated, post-estimation regime diagnostic detect genuine changes in a causal mechanism while rejecting spurious changes induced by model misspecification?
\item \textbf{RQ4} (\emph{generalization}): How does the model behave on problem families that were held out from its pretraining distribution?
\item \textbf{RQ5} (\emph{pathways}): Which computational pathways of the architecture actually produce the reported results?
\end{itemize}

The experimental results can be summarized as follows. On answerable questions, CWFM matches the best single fixed baseline in pooled error without knowing which mechanism generated each dataset, although a correctly chosen per-setting specialist remains somewhat better in individual settings (RQ1). It refuses every query whose declared identification or support conditions fail, while the baselines return a number in all of them (RQ2). Its calibrated regime diagnostic recovers most genuine mechanism changes and, unlike a linear splitting baseline, largely resists splitting a smooth nonlinear mechanism into spurious regimes (RQ3). Accuracy transfers to most problem families excluded from gradient training, with one instructive exception: the family qualitatively unlike the training distribution (RQ4). Finally, the reported estimates are produced entirely by the classical estimator blend selected by the pretrained router; the free-form neural components are inactive at the evaluated checkpoint, which we report openly (RQ5). A protocol-frozen confirmatory round (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory}) reproduces the accuracy, enforcement, and coverage results on fresh episode streams, verifies the repair of an invariance defect found in RQ5, and extends the evidence to a semi-synthetic benchmark on real energy-sensor covariates. It also shows that shallow non-neural aggregation baselines, tuned on a disjoint stream over the same expert library, match or slightly exceed the frozen router's accuracy.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Formal Identification and Automated Causal-Analysis Systems}\label{sec:related-causal}

Structural causal models and the potential-outcomes framework give causal questions a precise meaning~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. Their central tool is the theory of \emph{identification}: rules that determine whether a causal question can be answered from the available data under stated assumptions. When point identification fails, the theory does not stop; sensitivity analysis quantifies how conclusions change as hidden confounding grows~\cite{rosenbaum1983sensitivity}, partial identification replaces a point estimate with an honest interval~\cite{manski1990bounds}, and dedicated frameworks cover interference between units~\cite{hudgens2008interference}.

Identification is also an algorithmic problem. The identification algorithm of Shpitser and Pearl gives a complete graphical procedure for identifying arbitrary interventional distributions in semi-Markovian causal models~\cite{shpitser2006identification}. Building on such results, end-to-end libraries operationalize the full workflow: DoWhy separates modeling, identification, estimation, and refutation~\cite{sharma2020dowhy}, DoWhy-GCM exposes a broad family of graphical-causal-model queries through a common interface~\cite{blobaum2024dowhygcm}, and DECI combines causal discovery and effect inference in a single learned system~\cite{geffner2022deci}. Against this background, CWFM's scope is deliberately narrower: it evaluates a finite library of \emph{sufficient} identification conditions for its three implemented query classes (the randomized ATE, the observational ATE under a declared adjustment set, and network spillovers under a declared exposure mapping) rather than deriving arbitrary identifying functionals. Its gate is a contract validator, not a general identification engine; the contribution lies in combining this validation with empirical support checks and pretrained estimator routing behind one typed interface.

A complementary classical literature learns causal \emph{structure} from data, through constraint-based search~\cite{spirtes2000causation}, continuous optimization over graphs~\cite{zheng2018notears}, invariance across environments~\cite{peters2016invariant}, heterogeneous and nonstationary data~\cite{huang2020heterogeneous}, and nonlinear time series~\cite{runge2019pcmci}. These methods share the practical burden that motivates amortization: for every new dataset, an expert must choose the algorithm, the model class, and the tuning parameters without ground truth to check the choice.

\subsection{Amortized Causal-Effect Estimation}\label{sec:related-cfm}

\emph{Prior-data fitted networks} (PFNs) amortize inference: a transformer is pretrained on synthetic problems with known ground truth and afterwards processes new datasets without refitting, approximating Bayesian inference under its training prior~\cite{muller2022pfns,hollmann2025tabpfn}. The causal instances of this idea are the closest comparison group for this paper. CausalFM constructs valid priors for back-door, front-door, and instrumental-variable settings and predicts effects within the setting encoded by the prior~\cite{ma2025causalfm}. Do-PFN predicts interventional outcome distributions directly from observational data, with no graph required at inference~\cite{robertson2025dopfn}. CausalPFN amortizes average and heterogeneous treatment-effect estimation under ignorability encoded in its prior~\cite{balazadeh2025causalpfn}. ACTIVA targets full post-intervention distributions given an intervention query~\cite{sauter2025activa}. Partial-graph-conditioned models accept complete or partial causal knowledge as an input rather than fixing it in the prior~\cite{reuter2026partialgraphs}, and temporal and longitudinal variants extend the family to time-series and treatment-sequence data~\cite{thumm2026causaltimeprior,zare2026causallongpfn}.

These systems engage seriously with identification, but their published interfaces return an estimate \emph{within} the assumed or supplied causal setting; none exposes a runtime check that a specific submitted query satisfies explicit identification and support conditions, with a refusal when it does not. A second distinction concerns what is amortized. Direct causal PFNs amortize the mapping from a dataset to an effect or interventional distribution; CWFM instead amortizes the prediction of which per-dataset classical estimators are likely to perform well. Its learned object is an estimator router, not the effect estimator itself, and the evaluated checkpoint indeed obtains all point estimates from its classical library (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5}). Section~\ref{sec:assessment-comparison} situates CWFM among these systems at the capability level; we make no numerical cross-paper comparison, because the published results rest on different benchmarks, estimands, and outcome scalings. A related line amortizes causal-\emph{structure} prediction, forecasting graph skeletons, orderings, or edges~\cite{thompson2026arrow,li2026tabcausal,qiao2026cdfm,reuter2026partialgraphs}; graph discovery is relevant to CWFM's exploratory structural components but is not the evaluated task of this paper.

\subsection{Validation, Selection, and Aggregation of Causal Estimators}\label{sec:related-selection}

Causal model selection is difficult because the relevant counterfactual loss is not directly observed. Existing approaches construct proxy validation criteria from influence functions~\cite{alaa2019validating}, pseudo-outcomes and causal losses such as the R-learner objective~\cite{nie2021rlearner}, counterfactual cross-validation~\cite{saito2020counterfactualcv}, and selective machine learning for doubly robust functionals~\cite{cui2024selective}; empirical studies show that such selection metrics can rank candidate methods unreliably across settings~\cite{curth2023search,mahajan2024empirical}. Other work aggregates candidate treatment-effect models instead of selecting one, through ensembles of individualized-treatment-effect estimators~\cite{han2022ensemble} and causal Q-aggregation with model-selection guarantees~\cite{lan2024qaggregation}, and AutoCATE integrates the entire pipeline for end-to-end automated CATE estimation~\cite{vanderschueren2025autocate}.

CWFM builds on this literature, and its distinguishing element is where the aggregation weights come from. Existing validation and ensemble methods operate within the submitted dataset, because true causal error is unavailable there. CWFM instead trains an episode-level risk predictor across synthetic episodes for which every estimator's realized error is known, then applies that predictor to new episodes without updating its neural parameters, conditioned on a typed query and executed only after the contract and support checks have passed. Relative to AutoCATE specifically, AutoCATE performs per-dataset counterfactual pipeline optimization with a much richer CATE search space, whereas CWFM learns one cross-episode router before deployment, covers several typed query families, and places routing behind gates that can withhold the answer entirely. The current estimator library is deliberately small and mechanism-oriented: besides its g-computation variants it contains one doubly robust member, a cross-fitted augmented-IPW (AIPW) estimator with ridge-linear nuisance models (its network counterpart is a cross-fitted linear plug-in rather than a derived Neyman-orthogonal score). Extending the library with weighting-based, orthogonalized, and forest-based estimators such as TMLE, double machine learning, R-learners, and causal forests is a natural next step.

\subsection{Reliability under Non-Identification, Weak Support, and Prior Shift}\label{sec:related-reliability}

\paragraph{Positivity and empirical support.} Positivity is a population-level identification condition, whereas overlap diagnostics assess how well that condition is represented in a finite sample; in high dimensions, empirical overlap can deteriorate rapidly as covariates are added~\cite{damour2021overlap}. Existing responses to limited overlap include trimming to a better-supported subpopulation~\cite{crump2009overlap}, overlap weighting~\cite{li2018overlap}, redefining the target population, model-based extrapolation, and partial identification. These responses are not equivalent to refusal, because trimming and weighting change the target population and therefore the estimand. CWFM adopts a deliberately conservative policy: when its finite-sample support criterion fails, it refuses the originally requested estimand rather than silently changing the target population.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analysis and partial identification.} Refusal is not the only defensible output when point identification fails: depending on the setting, the appropriate answer can be an identified set~\cite{manski1990bounds}, a sensitivity curve indexed by an assumed confounding strength~\cite{rosenbaum1983sensitivity}, or an estimate for a restricted population, and amortized sensitivity analysis now makes some of these outputs cheap to produce~\cite{javurek2026sensitivitypfn}. CWFM refuses the \emph{requested point estimate under the supplied contract}; producing partial-identification outputs instead of refusals is a natural extension.

\paragraph{Prior-induced bias in causal PFNs.} Recent theory shows that causal PFNs interpreted as Bayesian ATE estimators can retain prior-induced confounding bias and fail to attain frequentist consistency without additional correction~\cite{ospc2026correction}, and a simulation study reports bias and poor credible-interval coverage in tested clinical-style scenarios~\cite{mourao2026pfnsim}. CWFM takes a different hybrid route: the pretrained model routes among per-dataset classical estimators rather than treating a PFN posterior as the causal estimate. The design still depends on the synthetic training prior, as the nonmonotone-interference result of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq4} demonstrates.

\paragraph{Conformal calibration and its target.} Conformal prediction provides finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability of calibration and future instances~\cite{vovk2005conformal,lei2018distribution}. The causal-conformal literature targets counterfactual outcomes and individual treatment effects~\cite{lei2021conformal}, calibration of heterogeneous-effect predictors~\cite{vanderlaan2023isotonic}, and intervals under causal-graph uncertainty~\cite{causalguard2026}. CWFM's guarantee is different in kind: it calibrates scalar episode-level estimation error across exchangeable simulator episodes, so coverage is marginal over the episode distribution of the calibration stream (Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}).

\paragraph{Four meanings of refusal.} Non-answers arise from logically distinct sources: (i)~\emph{logical non-identification}, where the supplied model and assumptions do not identify the estimand; (ii)~\emph{finite-sample unsupportedness}, where an identified estimand lacks adequate support in the submitted data; (iii)~\emph{learned high-risk abstention}, the selective-prediction setting that trades answer coverage against retained risk~\cite{geifman2017selective}; and (iv)~\emph{out-of-distribution rejection} of episodes unlike the training distribution. CWFM's refusal decisions are deterministic consequences of the declared contract and support policy, cases (i) and (ii). Learned reliability heads for pretrained causal models~\cite{tcpfn2026reliability} and self-compatibility checks~\cite{faller2024selfcompatibility} are complementary learned layers for cases (iii) and (iv), which CWFM does not yet cover.

\paragraph{Benchmarks that score non-answers.} Recent causal-reasoning benchmarks separate identification from numerical estimation and score whether a system declines underdetermined questions: CausalReasoningBenchmark evaluates identification specifications separately from estimation~\cite{sawarni2026crb}, CausalDS treats abstention as a scored outcome for data-science agents~\cite{leban2026causalds}, and CausalT5k evaluates structured refusal when evidence is underdetermined~\cite{geng2026causalt5k}. These benchmarks target language models or agents rather than estimator architectures; the shared principle, which CWFM operationalizes through deterministic machine-readable statuses, is that non-answer behavior should be evaluated explicitly.

\subsection{Task-Specific Foundations: Interference and Regime Determination}\label{sec:related-tasks}

\paragraph{Network interference.} Beyond the classical potential-outcomes treatment of interference~\cite{hudgens2008interference}, design-based frameworks connect the experimental design, the exposure mapping, and the causal estimand under general interference~\cite{aronow2017interference}; misspecified exposure mappings have their own estimability theory~\cite{savje2024misspecified}; and machine-learning estimators model network-mediated effects with learned representations~\cite{ma2021networked}. CWFM conditions on a user-supplied network and exposure mapping: its support gate checks whether the requested exposure contrast is represented in the submitted data, but it does not validate the network or the mapping itself, so exposure-mapping misspecification is a separate failure mode from insufficient support.

\paragraph{Regime determination.} What we call regime determination is closest to mechanism-change detection. Model-based recursive partitioning splits a fitted model along covariate thresholds~\cite{zeileis2008mob} and inspires our splitting baseline, a deliberately compact one-split variant (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-design}); causal trees seek subgroups with heterogeneous \emph{treatment effects}~\cite{athey2016recursive}; discovery from nonstationary data exploits mechanism change as a signal~\cite{huang2020heterogeneous}; and invariance-based methods test mechanism stability across environments~\cite{peters2016invariant}. CWFM's regime task asks whether the conditional outcome mechanism itself changes at a threshold. Its hard negatives distinguish a genuine piecewise mechanism from a smooth nonlinear mechanism that a partitioning method may approximate with multiple regions: a partition that improves predictive fit is not, by itself, evidence of a regime change.

\subsection{Positioning and Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

Each ingredient of CWFM exists somewhere in the literature reviewed above: formal identification, automated causal analysis, estimator validation and aggregation, conformal calibration, and scored non-answers. CWFM contributes a particular integration of these ideas. For a finite set of implemented query classes, it combines a typed causal query, deterministic validation of a declared assumption contract, an empirical-support policy, a pretrained cross-episode risk router over per-dataset classical estimators, episode-level conformal calibration, and typed non-answer outcomes, evaluated with refusal behavior as a first-class outcome. The contribution is reliability-gated estimator orchestration and its evaluation, not general graphical identification, automatic detection of hidden confounding, or out-of-distribution-safe abstention. \section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}

This section introduces the four concepts needed to follow the rest of the paper. Readers familiar with causal inference may skip to Section~\ref{sec:approach}.

\subsection{Structural Causal Models, Interventions, and Identification}\label{sec:prelim-scm}

A \emph{structural causal model} (SCM) describes a system as a set of variables, each generated by a mechanism from its direct causes plus independent noise~\cite{pearl2009causality}. As a running example, suppose a variable $X$ (say, the severity of a patient's condition) influences both a treatment decision $A$ and an outcome $Y$, and the treatment also influences the outcome. An \emph{intervention} replaces one mechanism by force: instead of letting doctors choose the treatment, we set it to a fixed value $a$, written $do(A=a)$. The canonical causal quantity in this paper is the \emph{average treatment effect} (ATE),
\begin{equation}
\tau \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid do(A=1)\right] \;-\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[Y \mid do(A=0)\right],
\end{equation}
the expected change in the outcome if everyone were treated versus no one. The difficulty is that we only observe data from the system \emph{without} the intervention: sicker patients are treated more often, so simply comparing treated and untreated outcomes mixes the effect of the treatment with the effect of severity.

\emph{Identification} asks whether a causal quantity can, in principle, be computed from the observed data under stated assumptions. In the example, if all common causes of $A$ and $Y$ are observed (here, $X$), the ATE is identified by the \emph{back-door adjustment} formula: compare treated and untreated outcomes within each level of $X$, then average over the population. Estimating this formula by fitting a regression of $Y$ on $A$ and $X$ and averaging its predictions is called \emph{g-computation}; it is one of the classical estimators our model builds on. If, however, some common cause is unobserved (\emph{hidden confounding}), no amount of data from the same source recovers the ATE: the question is \emph{not identified}, and an honest analysis must say so, widen its answer to a range, or state which additional assumptions would be needed.

\subsection{Network Interference and Exposure Mappings}\label{sec:prelim-interference}

Classical causal inference assumes that treating one unit does not affect another. In many settings this is false: vaccinating my neighbors protects me, and a promotion in one store draws customers from nearby stores. This phenomenon is called \emph{interference}, and its analysis requires knowing which units are connected, typically given as a network over the units~\cite{hudgens2008interference}. Because a unit can be affected by many neighbors in many ways, one summarizes the treatments of its neighbors by an \emph{exposure mapping}, for example ``the fraction of my neighbors that are treated.'' A typical causal quantity is then a \emph{spillover effect}: the expected change in my outcome if my neighbors' exposure moved from a low to a high level while my own treatment stayed fixed.

Two practical conditions matter throughout this paper. First, the exposure mapping is an assumption; if influence actually depends on, say, edge weights or two-hop neighbors, an analysis based on the simple fraction can be biased. Second, the requested contrast must have \emph{overlap} (also called positivity): there must exist comparable units observed at both exposure levels. If nobody in the data ever had a highly treated neighborhood, no method can estimate what would happen in one, and the only correct answer is to refuse.

\subsection{Regimes and Mechanism Changes}\label{sec:prelim-regimes}

Real systems are often not governed by one mechanism. The relation between inputs and outcome may differ across subpopulations, for example below and above a threshold of some observed variable: a machine behaves differently past a temperature limit, a subsidy rule changes at an income cutoff. We call such mechanism differences \emph{regimes}, and \emph{regime determination} is the task of deciding whether the data were generated by one mechanism or several, and if several, which variable and threshold separate them.

The task hides a trap that plays a central role in this paper. A single smooth nonlinear mechanism can be approximated surprisingly well by two linear pieces. A procedure that fits linear models and searches for splits will therefore ``find'' a regime boundary in perfectly stationary nonlinear data, and its predictive accuracy will genuinely improve. The improvement is real; the structural conclusion is false. Deciding between ``one curved mechanism'' and ``two straight ones'' therefore requires more than predictive gain, which is why regime determination is treated as a structural question, separate from prediction, throughout this work.

\subsection{Amortized Inference and Conformal Calibration}\label{sec:prelim-amortized}

Traditionally, every new dataset requires selecting and fitting a new statistical model. \emph{Amortized inference} moves this cost to a single pretraining phase: a neural network is trained on a very large collection of synthetic problems whose true answers are known by construction, and it learns to map a dataset directly to an answer~\cite{muller2022pfns}. At deployment, the pretrained network is applied to a new dataset without updating its parameters. The strength of this approach is that the network has effectively seen millions of analyses; its weakness is that everything it knows comes from the distribution of synthetic problems it was trained on, so that distribution acts as a prior and is part of the model's assumptions. CWFM adopts the pretraining idea for \emph{selection}: its neural parameters stay fixed at deployment, but the classical estimators it chooses among are still fitted to each new dataset.

A learned network also outputs uncertainty, but a neural confidence value carries no guarantee. \emph{Split-conformal prediction} provides one~\cite{vovk2005conformal,lei2018distribution}. The idea is simple: hold out a set of calibration problems the model has never used for training; record how wrong the model was on each; then widen the model's intervals on new problems until they cover the observed error distribution at the desired level, for example 90\%. Under mild conditions this yields intervals with a finite-sample validity guarantee, which is why, in this paper, all reported uncertainty comes from conformal calibration rather than from the network's raw confidence.

\section{Approach}\label{sec:approach}

CWFM takes a dataset together with a formal causal question and returns either a calibrated causal answer or an explicit, explained refusal. This section presents the architecture step by step. Every component exists to prevent one of the failures described in the introduction, and we state this motivation as each component is introduced.

\subsection{Design Principles and Overview}\label{sec:approach-overview}

Four principles shape the design.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Amortize the choice of method, not the method itself.} The model learns to select and combine well-understood classical estimators. It does not replace them with a free-form neural predictor.
\item \emph{A learned correction must earn its place.} A checkpoint is eligible only if its neural correction does not materially degrade the classical compiled estimate on designated development data; when in doubt, the classical estimate stands.
\item \emph{Decide whether the question is answerable before trying to answer it.} This decision is made by deterministic logic, not by the network.
\item \emph{Report only uncertainty that carries a guarantee, and refuse rather than guess.} Intervals come from conformal calibration; unanswerable questions receive a refusal, never a number.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows how these principles translate into a pipeline. The input is a \emph{causal episode} (the data plus everything known about it) together with a formal query that acts as a declared contract (Section~\ref{sec:approach-episode}). Before any estimation, two deterministic gates check whether the query satisfies the implemented identification and support conditions; if not, the pipeline exits with a structured refusal (Section~\ref{sec:approach-gates}). If the gates pass, a library of classical estimators is fitted to the episode, and a typed encoder with a pretrained risk router blends their candidate estimates into one answer (Sections~\ref{sec:approach-encoder} and \ref{sec:approach-estimation}). Conformal calibration then turns the answer into an interval with a stated coverage property (Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}). Two further components, structural world-particle scores and a gated neural residual, are implemented but presently exploratory (Sections~\ref{sec:approach-worlds} and \ref{sec:approach-estimation}). Section~\ref{sec:approach-data} describes the training and evaluation data, Section~\ref{sec:approach-training} the pretraining and the safety-constrained model selection, and Section~\ref{sec:approach-guarantees} states precisely what is and is not guaranteed.

\subsection{Causal Episodes and the Formal Query Language}\label{sec:approach-episode}

The input unit of CWFM is a \emph{causal episode}: the dataset together with everything relevant that is known about it. Concretely, an episode contains the observed values, indicators of which values are missing, a schema stating the type of each variable (continuous, binary, and so on), the role of each variable with respect to the question (treatment, outcome, or covariate), an optional relation matrix connecting the units when interference is possible, and declared design knowledge, for example that the treatment was randomized. Passing this context explicitly matters: whether an analysis is valid often depends less on the numbers than on how they were produced.

The question itself is a \emph{formal query}, a small structured object stating the claim type (associational or interventional), the treatment and outcome variables, the causal quantity requested (for example the ATE, or a spillover contrast between two exposure levels), and the target population. We insist on a formal query rather than a natural-language question for two reasons. First, small changes of wording must not silently change the quantity being estimated; the formal object makes the estimand explicit and fixed. Second, the claim type prevents a quiet upgrade of an associational finding into a causal one: an interventional answer can only be produced for a query that requested one and passed the gates. A language model may translate a scientist's question into this format, but the causal core operates on the structured object only.

Together, the query and the declared design knowledge form a \emph{contract}, and the whole system can be summarized as one function
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(E, Q) \;\rightarrow\; \bigl(S,\; \widehat{\tau},\; C_\alpha,\; R,\; D\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $E$ is the episode, $Q$ the formal query, and $S$ a status taking one of four values: \texttt{answer}, \texttt{not identified}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{out of scope}. The point estimate $\widehat{\tau}$ and the calibrated interval $C_\alpha$ are present only when $S = \texttt{answer}$; $R$ is a machine-readable refusal reason, and $D$ contains diagnostics that inform the user but carry no guarantee. The three non-answer statuses are deliberately distinct: a query can be formally nonidentified under the declared assumptions, it can be identified in principle but outside the rule library implemented so far, or it can be identified but lack adequate finite-sample support. \subsection{Typed Encoder and Mechanism Experts}\label{sec:approach-encoder}

The encoder converts an episode into internal representations. Each cell of the data table is embedded from its value, its missingness indicator, the type of its variable, and the role of its variable in the query. The per-cell encoding deliberately contains no embedding of the column position: the meaning of a variable should come from its role and type, not from being column four. The query vector does supply the normalized column indices of the treatment, outcome, and exposure variables, so the accurate characterization is that the architecture is \emph{trained under presentation randomization} (Section~\ref{sec:approach-data}) rather than blind to column positions; Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5} measures the practical consequences.

\emph{Attention} is the basic operation of transformer models: each part of the input gathers information from the parts related to it. In CWFM, attention alternates along two axes. Attention across variables, within one sample, lets the model relate treatment, outcome, and covariates to one another. Attention across samples, within one variable, lets it see distributional patterns such as skewness, subgroup structure, or outliers. When the episode contains a relation matrix, an additional message-passing step lets each unit see summaries of its neighbors, which is what a spillover analysis needs.

The feed-forward layers are organized as a sparse \emph{mixture of mechanism experts}. Instead of one universal network, several small expert networks specialize in different mechanism families, and a learned gate activates only the most relevant ones for each episode. We call this the \emph{encoder expert gate}, to keep it clearly distinct from the estimator risk router of Section~\ref{sec:approach-estimation}: the former shapes internal representations, the latter chooses among fitted classical estimators. The gate carries a simplicity prior, so flexible experts are used only when the data demand them; in our benchmarks, a simple linear method beats a flexible one when the truth is linear, and the flexible method wins when the truth has a threshold, a distinction that a single uniformly flexible pathway tends to blur. Figure~\ref{fig:encoder} summarizes the encoder.

\subsection{Structural Heads and Exploratory World Particles}\label{sec:approach-worlds}

Structural decoders read the encoder representations and output probabilities rather than hard decisions. The most important one is the \emph{regime decoder}. It is hierarchical and mirrors how an analyst would reason: first, is there evidence of a mechanism change at all; if so, which variable separates the regimes; at which threshold; and what kind of change it is. Because the stationary nonlinear trap of Section~\ref{sec:prelim-regimes} is the known failure mode here, the decoder is trained on matched pairs of episodes in which one curved mechanism and two straight ones look predictively similar but differ structurally. One point of standing matters for reading Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq3}: at the evaluated checkpoint, the reported regime decision does not come from this learned decoder but from thresholding a compiled, BIC-based evidence statistic against a threshold fitted on the calibration bank (Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}); the learned decoder's outputs are recorded as diagnostics only (Figure~\ref{fig:regime}d).

The architecture also contains \emph{world particles}: a small set of scored hypotheses about the data-generating process, each combining a graph, a mechanism family, and a regime hypothesis. The design goal, which gives the model its name, is to separate uncertainty about \emph{which structure} generated the data from uncertainty about \emph{parameter values} within a structure. This module is implemented but exploratory: the final point estimate does not depend on the particle scores at the evaluated checkpoint, and Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5} shows they remained uninformative in this run. Figure~\ref{fig:regime} summarizes the regime pathway.

\subsection{Identification and Support Gates}\label{sec:approach-gates}

Before any estimate is produced, two gates decide whether the query should be answered at all.

The \emph{identification gate} checks whether the requested causal quantity is identified given the episode's declared assumptions and design knowledge, for example randomized assignment or the assumption that all relevant confounders are observed. This gate is deterministic logic, not a learned component, for a principled reason: identifiability is a property of the causal model and its assumptions and cannot be certified from data patterns alone. A learned score could only imitate the training prior and would produce exactly the confident wrong answers we aim to prevent. This gate is the direct response to the hidden-confounding failure of the introduction.

The \emph{support gate} checks whether the requested contrast is present in the data: are there comparable units observed under both treatment values, or both exposure levels, for the population in the query? Two related notions must be kept apart. \emph{Population positivity}, the assumption that the contrast occurs with positive probability in the population, is an identification assumption and belongs to the contract. \emph{Observed finite-sample support}, which the deployed gate measures, is an empirical statistic of the submitted dataset compared against a threshold. The gate enforces the second: if too few comparable observations exist, any estimate would be extrapolation rather than evidence, and the output is suppressed. One further distinction bounds what the benchmark shows. The public application runtime (Section~\ref{sec:tools}) computes the support statistic from the submitted data; in the locked-bank experiment of Section~\ref{sec:assessment}, the support condition is instead supplied through the episode's declared support class, exactly like the identification condition. The experiments therefore measure enforcement of declared conditions, while the finite-sample operating characteristics of the empirical support statistic remain to be benchmarked separately.

When a gate fails, CWFM returns a \emph{structured refusal} whose reason code distinguishes the three cases introduced in Section~\ref{sec:approach-episode}: formally nonidentified under the declared assumptions, outside the implemented rule library, or inadequately supported in this sample. The refusal also names what would resolve it, for example an instrument, an experiment, or additional data in the unsupported region. Learned diagnostic heads accompany the gates with empirical signals such as estimated overlap and prior mismatch, but they can only add caution: they can never overrule a gate and force an answer. Figure~\ref{fig:gates} shows the complete decision logic with concrete examples.

\subsection{A Risk-Routed Library of Classical Estimators}\label{sec:approach-estimation}

When both gates pass, the answer is produced by classical statistics guided by the learned model, in three steps.

First, every member of a \emph{library of estimators} that is compatible with the query class (Table~\ref{tab:queryclasses}) is fitted to the episode's data, each producing its own candidate estimate; structurally incompatible estimators are masked out. Each member is a standard method that is correct under a different mechanism class: linear g-computation, a cross-fitted augmented-IPW (doubly robust) estimator with ridge-linear nuisance models, g-computation with interactions, a spline-based version, a piecewise (hinge-basis) exposure-response estimator for threshold spillovers, and a design-based estimator for randomized episodes with a null estimator that returns no effect. In the network library, the slot corresponding to the AIPW expert is a cross-fitted linear plug-in estimator rather than a derived Neyman-orthogonal score. Cross-fitting is used for the nuisance-estimation pathways, with cluster-level folds for network episodes. This step is genuine per-episode statistical fitting: what stays fixed at deployment are the neural parameters, not the estimators.

Second, the \emph{estimator risk router} predicts, from the episode representation, how large each estimator's error is likely to be on this particular episode, and blends the candidates accordingly:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{compiled}} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{J} w_j(E, Q)\,\widehat{\tau}_j,
\qquad \sum_{j} w_j = 1,\quad w_j \geq 0,
\end{equation}
where the weights are obtained from the predicted risks of the compatible estimators. The router is supervised during pretraining: because every synthetic episode carries its true effect, the realized error of every estimator is observable there, and the router learns to predict it. We call $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{compiled}}$ the \emph{compiled estimate}. It is the answer classical statistics would give if an experienced analyst had chosen and blended the estimators; the router amortizes that choice. Figure~\ref{fig:router} traces the full computation on a schematic example, including the compatibility mask and the gated residual introduced next.

Third, a neural \emph{residual} may adjust the compiled estimate. It is initialized at zero, passes through a learned gate, and is trained with a \emph{no-harm objective} that penalizes any adjustment that worsens the compiled estimate far more than it rewards an improvement; checkpoint eligibility additionally requires that the residual does not materially degrade the compiled estimate on the development banks. If the residual cannot prove its worth during training, the gate stays closed and the final answer equals the compiled estimate. This is the architectural embodiment of principle 2: the burden of proof is on the neural correction, never on the classical estimate.

\subsection{Calibration}\label{sec:approach-calibration}

All reported uncertainty comes from split-conformal calibration (Section~\ref{sec:prelim-amortized}). A dedicated bank of calibration episodes, never used for training, records how wrong the model's answers are in practice; intervals on new episodes are then widened until they cover this error distribution at the target level. The conformal scores are normalized and stratified so that intervals adapt to episode difficulty: an episode routed to a risky estimator, or one where the estimators disagree, receives a wider interval (whether the normalizing scale tracks realized difficulty is an exploratory design choice rather than a validated property). Every baseline is calibrated on the same bank, at the same coverage target, and with the same finite-sample quantile rule; the baselines use ordinary absolute-residual radii rather than CWFM's normalized score, so the coverage comparison of Section~\ref{sec:assessment} shares the calibration data and target but not the score construction. The threshold at which the regime decision declares a mechanism change is fitted on the same calibration bank rather than learned end to end, for the same reason: a decision with error-rate consequences should be set where its error rate can be measured. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} shows how one calibration bank supports both decisions and where the guarantee stops.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Data}\label{sec:approach-data}

The data deserve particular attention, because for a pretrained causal model the training distribution is not an implementation detail: it is part of the model's assumptions. Everything CWFM knows, it knows from this distribution.

All training and evaluation data are \emph{synthetic causal episodes} produced by a simulator, a necessity rather than a convenience: supervising a causal model requires labels that real data cannot provide, namely the true effect of an intervention, the true causal structure, and the true answer to the question ``should this query be refused?''. In a simulation these labels are known by construction. Each episode is created in three steps: a complete causal world is sampled (a graph, mechanisms, an assignment process, and an effect size); the world is run to produce an observed dataset; and the ground-truth labels are recorded and hidden from the model, which must recover them from the data alone.

Episodes come from three task families, matching the implemented scope:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Static treatment effects.} Between 64 and 128 units are described by three to eight covariates; a binary treatment affects a continuous outcome. Scenarios include linear and nonlinear mechanisms with observed confounding, and randomized experiments.
\item \emph{Regime determination.} Datasets with five to nine covariates whose outcome mechanism either is stationary, splits at a threshold of one covariate (with strong or deliberately weak shifts), or is a stationary smooth nonlinear mechanism, the hard negative of Section~\ref{sec:prelim-regimes}. Exact nulls are always included.
\item \emph{Network interference.} Units are linked by a cluster-structured network and treated cluster by cluster with varying saturation; each unit's exposure is the treated fraction of its neighbors. Spillover mechanisms are linear, threshold-shaped, or exactly null.
\end{itemize}
Crucially, every family also generates \emph{unanswerable episodes} in which the correct output is a refusal: hidden confounding (an unobserved variable drives both treatment and outcome) and poor overlap or poor support (the requested contrast barely occurs in the data). These cases are mixed into training at substantial rates. A model never shown them would learn that producing an answer is always rewarded, which is precisely the behavior we must avoid.

To prevent the model from learning superficial shortcuts, the presentation of every training episode is randomized: rows are shuffled, columns are permuted while preserving their declared roles, covariate scales are varied, and a random subset of covariate values (up to five percent) is masked as missing. The model must therefore rely on the declared roles and types of variables, never on their position or scale.

Training and evaluation use five strictly separated streams, drawn from disjoint random-seed ranges so that no episode can appear in more than one stream. Figure~\ref{fig:streams} shows the generation process, the wall between what the model sees and what only the evaluator sees, and the separation logic of the streams; Table~\ref{tab:streams} summarizes their contents. Training episodes are generated online and never repeated: over 1,600 steps with 24 episodes per batch, the full optimization run consumes 38,400 distinct episodes exactly once each. The final test bank is \emph{locked}: it is evaluated once, after all training, selection, and calibration decisions are frozen, and its 14 scenario cells with 30 seeds each are identical for CWFM and for every baseline.

The out-of-distribution development bank deserves a separate remark. It consists of entire mechanism and graph families that are excluded from training: hinge, multiplicative, and saturating outcome mechanisms; intercept, variance, and shifted regime changes; two random-network families unlike the cluster networks seen in training; and nonmonotone spillovers. \subsection{Pretraining and Safety-Constrained Model Selection}\label{sec:approach-training}

CWFM is pretrained on the online episode stream of Section~\ref{sec:approach-data}, and training proceeds in stages. The estimator library and the router are trained first, with the residual frozen, so that a sound compiled path exists before any neural correction is allowed. The gated residual is then trained under the no-harm objective, and finally the whole system is fine-tuned jointly at a low learning rate.

Model selection enforces the reliability requirements as \emph{eligibility constraints}, not as terms in a loss. A checkpoint is eligible only if, on the designated development banks, its residual does not materially degrade the compiled estimate, it keeps the false-regime rate on stationary nonlinear episodes below a cap, and it keeps an acceptable number of estimator experts in active use. Refusal behavior is deliberately not among these criteria: it is enforced at run time by the deterministic gates of Section~\ref{sec:approach-gates}, which no learned component can bypass, so it holds for every checkpoint rather than being selected for. Only the eligible checkpoints are then ranked, by their worst-group error on the held-out development families; a checkpoint with better average accuracy that violates any constraint is discarded. This ordering, reliability first and accuracy second, prevents the selection process itself from trading reliability for benchmark scores. Figure~\ref{fig:selection} shows the staged timeline, the eligibility funnel, and the trade-off actually observed: later checkpoints scored better unconstrained but exceeded the false-split cap.

One consequence of this selection shapes how the results of Section~\ref{sec:assessment} should be read. The selected checkpoint, step 100, lies inside the \emph{first} training stage: it was saved before the residual-training stage (steps 241 to 1040) and the joint fine-tuning stage began, so the residual head and the world-effect mixture objective had received no training in the evaluated parameters, which reflect the first 2,400 training episodes. The evaluated system is therefore best interpreted as a pretrained estimator router over a classical library; no empirical claim in this paper concerns the behavior of a trained residual (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5}).

\section{Assessment}\label{sec:assessment}

This section first describes the experimental design, then answers the five research questions of the introduction, one subsection each, adding after RQ1 a contextual comparison with published causal foundation models. Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory} reports a second, protocol-frozen evaluation round on fresh episode streams, and the section closes with qualitative case studies that complement the aggregate metrics with worked examples.

\subsection{Experimental Design}\label{sec:assessment-design}

All results below come from the locked final test bank of Section~\ref{sec:approach-data}, which is evaluated exactly once, after every training, selection, and calibration decision is frozen. The bank contains 14 scenario cells with 30 seeds each, identical for all methods. Six cells contain answerable effect queries (static linear, static nonlinear, and randomized treatment effects; linear, threshold, and null network spillovers), giving 180 queries in which the true effect is known and an estimate is expected. Four cells contain regime-determination problems (a strong split, a weak split, the stationary nonlinear hard negative, and a complete null). The remaining four cells are \emph{stress cells} with 120 queries that must be refused: hidden confounding and poor overlap in the static setting, and hidden confounding and poor support in the network setting.

CWFM is compared against five baselines that always answer: linear g-computation, g-computation with interactions, spline g-computation, a piecewise exposure-response estimator, and random forest g-computation. Each baseline is a representative specialist for at least one of the implemented mechanism cells, so the comparison is deliberately unfavorable to a general model. The baseline set and CWFM's internal library overlap but differ: random forest g-computation represents a generic flexible fit and is not one of CWFM's experts, while the design-based and null experts inside CWFM are not meaningful stand-alone baselines. For regime determination, the baseline is a compact splitting procedure in the spirit of model-based recursive partitioning~\cite{zeileis2008mob}: a BIC-penalized linear model with at most one split, called the \emph{one-split linear baseline} throughout (conclusions about it do not extend to richer partitioning methods with nonlinear base models). We also report \emph{CWFM (compiled only)}, the full system with the neural residual removed; the difference between the two isolates the contribution of the learned correction in RQ5.

One provenance clarification is needed for RQ4. The six held-out families evaluated there were excluded from all gradient training, but they also form the out-of-distribution development bank used to rank eligible checkpoints (Table~\ref{tab:streams}), and the per-family values rest on four development episodes per family. RQ4 is therefore presented as a development diagnostic rather than as confirmatory evidence. The implementation is modest in scale: the encoder uses hidden dimension 96 with three attention blocks and four mechanism experts, the estimator library has six members, and the full training run takes about half an hour on one GPU.

Metrics are the mean absolute error (MAE) of the estimated effect against the known truth, the empirical coverage and width of the 90\% intervals, the rate of false answers on the stress cells, and structure accuracy for regime determination (the method must select the correct split variable, or correctly select no split; threshold localization is not scored). Differences between methods are assessed with paired bootstrap confidence intervals over the shared seeds. All seeds, configuration values, and package versions are recorded in a result manifest together with a cryptographic hash of the evaluated checkpoint, so the experiment can be reproduced exactly.

\subsection{RQ1: Accuracy on Answerable Queries}\label{sec:assessment-rq1}

\emph{Answer: as a pooled generalist, yes. CWFM matches the best single fixed baseline in pooled error without knowing which setting it is in, and clearly outperforms the generic flexible baseline; a per-cell oracle of specialists remains ahead.}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} shows the pooled picture over the 180 answerable queries. CWFM reaches a pooled MAE of 0.166, side by side with the two best fixed baselines (interaction g-computation at 0.167 and linear g-computation at 0.169) and far ahead of random forest g-computation at 0.329. The paired intervals contain zero, so the benchmark does not detect a difference between CWFM and the two best fixed baselines: CWFM versus linear g-computation is $-0.0028$ with a 95\% bootstrap interval of $[-0.0132, 0.0080]$, and versus interaction g-computation $-0.0003$ with $[-0.0111, 0.0109]$. Versus random forest, the difference is $-0.163$ with $[-0.203, -0.122]$, a clear improvement. Coverage of the 90\% intervals is 0.906 pooled, and the mean interval width (0.793) is in the same range as the fixed baselines (0.772 to 0.817) and far below random forest (1.337). The pooled coverage hides subgroup heterogeneity: by cell, coverage lies between 0.900 and 0.967 everywhere except the network-threshold cell, which reaches only 0.700 (21 of 30; 95\% Wilson interval 0.52 to 0.83), exactly where routing is weakest (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5}). The conformal guarantee is episode-marginal and promises nothing per cell, but the undercoverage should be reported rather than averaged away; per-cell calibration is an evident next step.

\subsection{RQ2: Enforcement of the Declared Contract}\label{sec:assessment-rq2}

\emph{Answer: yes. CWFM answered all 180 queries whose declared contracts were valid and refused all 120 queries whose declared identification or support conditions fail, without the learned components ever bypassing the gates. Every baseline returned a number in every stress cell.}

One fact frames the interpretation: the identification and support status of each stress episode is supplied through its declared metadata (Section~\ref{sec:approach-gates}). CWFM does not infer the existence of an unobserved confounder from the observed data; no method can do so from the same observational distribution alone. What RQ2 tests is whether the runtime gate correctly enforces the declared conditions and whether the surrounding learned machinery ever circumvents it.

The stress cells are the queries where a number is a mistake under the declared contract: declared hidden confounding makes the effect unidentifiable, and poor overlap or support means the requested contrast barely occurs in the data. The always-answer baselines produce estimates in all of them, and those estimates inherit exactly the failure described in the introduction: they can look precise while being systematically wrong. CWFM instead returned a structured refusal naming the failed condition in every case. Two qualifications bound the claim. First, false metadata, an incomplete gate rule, or a mechanism outside the prior can still lead to a wrong answer; the gates enforce the contract, they do not verify it. Second, the same gating layer could in principle wrap any estimator, so the comparison with ungated baselines demonstrates the value of the wrapper rather than a unique property of CWFM's point estimator. The contract-compliance property test of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory} makes both points measurable at larger scale.

\subsection{RQ3: Regime Detection without False Splits}\label{sec:assessment-rq3}

\emph{Answer: largely yes. The calibrated evidence rule recovers most strong regime splits while rejecting most spurious ones, where the one-split linear baseline is fooled every time; weak splits remain out of reach for both.}

As stated in Section~\ref{sec:approach-worlds}, the decision evaluated here is produced by CWFM's compiled, BIC-based evidence statistic thresholded on the calibration bank; the learned split head is recorded as a diagnostic only, so RQ3 validates an evidence-calibrated classical rule executed inside CWFM's pipeline, not learned structure discovery. Table~\ref{tab:regime} compares this diagnostic (reported as CWFM) with the one-split linear baseline. Structure accuracy requires selecting the correct split variable, or correctly selecting no split; threshold localization is not scored, because the generator draws the true threshold continuously while the decision selects among five quantile candidates, and the baseline reports no comparable threshold. On strong splits the baseline is more sensitive (structure accuracy 0.800 versus 0.633). The decisive difference is the stationary nonlinear hard negative: the baseline declares a split in 100\% of these episodes, mistaking one curved mechanism for two regimes in every run, while CWFM declares a split in only 16.7\% and identifies the correct stationary structure in 83.3\%. Both methods are essentially perfect on the complete null, and both miss the deliberately weak splits, whose signal is simply too small at this sample size. Pooled over all four cells, CWFM reaches 0.617 structure accuracy versus 0.458 for the baseline. Trading some sensitivity for a large gain in structural specificity is intentional and follows from the calibrated decision threshold of Section~\ref{sec:approach-calibration}.

\subsection{RQ4: Generalization to Held-Out Families}\label{sec:assessment-rq4}

\emph{Answer: mixed, and best read as a development diagnostic. Accuracy transfers to most held-out families but degrades sharply on the one family qualitatively unlike anything in training, and the system did not recognize the mismatch.}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq4} shows effect MAE on the six mechanism and graph families excluded from gradient training, with the provenance limits stated in Section~\ref{sec:assessment-design}: four episodes per family, which also informed checkpoint selection. The qualitative pattern is clear. On hinge outcomes, multiplicative outcomes, and the unfamiliar Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi random graphs, error stays near the in-distribution level; saturating outcomes and small-world graphs sit substantially above it; and nonmonotone interference reaches an MAE of 1.357, roughly eight times the in-distribution level. Spillovers that first rise and then fall resemble nothing in the training distribution, so the model has no basis for them. An independent, adequately sized out-of-distribution test bank, never used in checkpoint selection, is required before RQ4 can be answered confirmatorily.

\subsection{RQ5: Which Pathways Are Active?}\label{sec:assessment-rq5}

\emph{Answer: the point predictions are generated entirely by the compiled classical estimator blend. The neural residual and the world-particle module make no measurable contribution at the evaluated checkpoint, which precedes their training stage.}

The cleanest evidence is the comparison with CWFM (compiled only): the two systems produce point estimates that are equal to numerical precision. The gated neural residual remains almost closed (mean gate activation 0.0067) and its mean absolute correction is below $3\times10^{-8}$. Because the selected checkpoint precedes the residual-training stage (Section~\ref{sec:approach-training}), this near-zero correction is a property of an untrained, gated pathway: the experiment establishes that the untrained residual did not perturb the compiled estimate, not whether the no-harm objective can deliver safe improvements. The same reading applies to the world-particle scores, which remained effectively uniform in this run. This analysis identifies which pathways are active; it does not ablate the estimator library, the router, and the gating logic separately.

Routing works but is not sharp. All six estimator experts stay in use, yet in threshold spillover episodes the estimator risk router assigns the piecewise expert only about 15\% of the weight although that expert is clearly the best there (Table~\ref{tab:scenario}: 0.173 versus 0.283 for CWFM's blend); the same pattern appears in the network-null cell, where the null-shrinkage expert is exact yet likewise receives only about 15\% of the weight, so CWFM reports 0.100 instead of zero. Sharper episode-level risk prediction is the most direct path to improvement, and the oracle-within-library gap of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq1} bounds the available headroom.

Finally, Table~\ref{tab:probes} reports presentation-change probes: the estimate should not change when the data are presented differently. Each value is measured on a single static linear ATE probe episode, so these are illustrative diagnostics rather than averages over a probe bank. Row shuffling and rescaling the outcome by a positive factor change the estimate only at numerical noise level. Inserting an irrelevant covariate shifts it moderately (a stability probe rather than a strict invariance, since the data genuinely change), and reordering columns with roles preserved shifts it most: the observed change of 0.170 is of the same order as the pooled estimation MAE of 0.166. The sensitivity is traceable to the classical estimator layer rather than to the encoder: the cross-fitting fold assignment ordered rows lexicographically by the covariate columns, and the hinge-basis expert constructed its basis from the first three covariates only. After this locked evaluation, both root causes were repaired in the classical estimator layer, without updating any neural parameter, and all conformal radii were recalibrated on fresh streams; the frozen invariance bank of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory} verifies the repair at scale. Together with the diffuse routing and the untrained exploratory modules, these probes gave the confirmatory round a concrete agenda.

\subsection{Protocol-Frozen Confirmatory, Aggregation-Baseline, and External Evaluation}\label{sec:assessment-confirmatory}

\emph{Summary: on 1,800 fresh episodes the frozen router reproduces the RQ1 picture, statistically indistinguishable from the best fixed expert and clearly better than uniform averaging, while two shallow non-neural aggregators tuned on a disjoint stream exceed it by small, statistically resolvable margins. Coverage is near nominal for every method under one shared conformal protocol, the gates wrap all 13 deployable estimators identically on 1,200 stress episodes, the invariance repair of RQ5 is verified, and the qualitative ordering transfers to a semi-synthetic benchmark on real energy-sensor covariates.}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The evaluation-methodology steps predeclared as future work in the first version of this paper were executed as a single follow-up experiment. Its protocol (seed streams, method list, hyperparameter policy, coverage target, and bootstrap plan) was frozen in a version-controlled file (\path{experiments/revision_protocol.json}) before the confirmatory streams were inspected, and the result manifest records its hash together with the SHA-256 of the evaluated checkpoint, which is byte-identical to the released one: no neural parameter was updated at any point. The estimator-layer invariance repair of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5} was applied before any new stream was drawn, so every method below shares the corrected classical layer. Four disjoint synthetic seed streams cover the six answerable scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-design}: an aggregation-tuning stream (1,200 episodes) on which all tunable aggregation policies are fitted, a calibration stream (1,800 episodes) used only for conformal radii, a confirmatory test stream (1,800 episodes; 300 per scenario, a tenfold increase over the locked bank), and a stress stream (1,200 episodes) for the contract property test. An invariance bank and an external benchmark complete the round.

\paragraph{Baseline matrix.} Every method is evaluated under identical conditions. The deployable methods comprise each library expert as a stand-alone estimator; four conventional comparators (linear and random-forest g-computation, cross-fitted AIPW with ridge-linear nuisances, and a cross-fitted doubly robust learner with spline outcome and propensity nuisances); and nine aggregation policies over the same fitted expert library: the released frozen-router soft blend, hard top-1 routing from the frozen logits, a temperature-scaled variant of those logits (temperature tuned per task on the tuning stream), the uniform mean and the median of compatible experts, the best fixed expert selected on the tuning stream, a metadata-only ridge risk selector, a shallow random-forest risk selector reading the fitted experts' estimates and standard errors alongside the metadata, and a task-wise nonnegative convex stack. Per-cell and per-episode oracles are reported as non-deployable upper bounds. Uncertainty uses ordinary absolute-residual split conformal around each method's point estimate, scenario-stratified, with the exact finite-sample quantile, the same 90\% target, and the same calibration stream for every deployable method: the protocol-identical comparison that RQ1 could not offer.

\paragraph{Accuracy.} Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory} gives the confirmatory ordering. The RQ1 conclusions replicate at the higher resolution: the frozen soft blend (pooled MAE 0.161) is statistically indistinguishable from the best fixed expert (0.163), from hard top-1 routing, and from the metadata-only selector, and it is significantly better than the uniform expert mean. The best stand-alone experts land at the same level (linear and interaction g-computation, both 0.163), while the conventional flexible comparators trail at these sample sizes (doubly robust spline learner 0.211, random-forest g-computation 0.314, cross-fitted linear-nuisance AIPW 0.373). The new finding is at the top of the table: the shallow risk selector (0.141) and the convex stack (0.147) improve on the frozen router by paired margins of $+0.020$ $[0.015, 0.025]$ and $+0.014$ $[0.011, 0.017]$, and even the median of compatible experts and a one-parameter temperature rescaling of the router's own logits are significantly, if marginally, better. The worst-cell column shows where the difference is earned: in the threshold-spillover scenario, the router's known weak spot, the stack (0.179) and the shallow selector (0.191) come close to the piecewise specialist while the router stays at 0.286. That temperature scaling helps indicates the frozen logits rank the experts sensibly but are too diffuse; that a shallow random forest over expert estimates, standard errors, and episode metadata does substantially better indicates the routing task itself is learnable from cheap per-episode features. The oracle rows show the headroom that remains for all methods.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Under the shared absolute-residual protocol, pooled coverage of every deployable method lies between 0.879 and 0.940 at the 0.9 target; the frozen router attains 0.886 with a 95\% Wilson interval of $[0.871, 0.900]$, and the shallow selector and stack attain 0.888 and 0.894. The residual undercoverage is concentrated in the static nonlinear scenario (0.82 to 0.87 for several methods), consistent with the per-cell heterogeneity reported in RQ1 and again arguing for per-scenario calibration budgets rather than a different score.

\paragraph{Contract compliance as a property test.} On the 1,200 stress episodes (poor overlap and hidden confounding, static and network), all 13 deployable methods were evaluated twice: in always-answer form and wrapped by the identical declared-contract gate. The always-answer forms produced an estimate in every episode; the gate-wrapped forms refused in every episode; and in all 15,600 method and episode combinations the gate decision matched the declared contract. This is the property-test formulation of RQ2: enforcement is exact, deterministic, and estimator-independent, and, as before, it is enforcement of supplied contract fields rather than detection of hidden confounding.

\paragraph{Invariance bank.} The frozen robustness bank evaluates the full pipeline on 300 episodes per transformation (150 for treatment-label recoding, which applies only to eligible episodes). After the estimator-layer repair, the transformations that should be exact are exact to numerical precision: row permutation, covariate permutation, and outcome translation change the estimate by at most $2.9\times10^{-6}$, $2.4\times10^{-7}$, and $2.4\times10^{-7}$ respectively, and positive outcome rescaling by at most $8.4\times10^{-5}$, against the single-episode shift of 0.170 that column reordering produced before the repair (Table~\ref{tab:probes}). Across the whole bank, 97.25\% of probes pass at a $10^{-5}$ tolerance. The remaining sensitivities are stability rather than strict-invariance findings: inserting an irrelevant covariate changes the estimate by 0.019 on average (maximum 0.278), negative outcome rescaling by 0.023, and treatment-label recoding by 0.010.

\paragraph{External semi-synthetic benchmark.} The first step outside the simulator uses the UCI Appliances Energy Prediction data~\cite{candanedo2017appliances} (DOI 10.24432/C5VC8G, CC~BY~4.0). Eight temperature and humidity covariates, selected without reference to the outcome, are standardized; the prognostic response is the standardized logarithm of appliance energy use; and a fixed confounded logistic treatment assignment and a heterogeneous effect mechanism, both functions of the real covariates only, were committed to code before any evaluation, so the true episode-level ATE is known while the covariate distribution is real. Episodes of 128 units are drawn from even source rows for calibration (200 episodes) and odd source rows for testing (300 episodes), with rows and columns randomly permuted per episode. The qualitative ordering transfers: the linear expert is best (0.157), the stack (0.160), temperature-scaled router (0.162), median (0.163), and shallow selector (0.165) follow, and the frozen soft blend reaches 0.171, significantly behind the temperature-scaled, median, and stack aggregators and again significantly better than the uniform mean. Coverage remains near nominal for all methods (0.883 to 0.930; frozen router 0.913). One domain with a synthetic treatment layer does not establish real-world validity, but it shows that the gates, the calibration protocol, and the relative ordering of aggregators survive first contact with real covariate structure.

\paragraph{What this round changes.} The confirmatory evidence strengthens every reliability claim of the paper (enforcement, calibration, invariance after repair) and refines one accuracy claim: at this checkpoint, the frozen pretrained router is matched or slightly exceeded by shallow non-neural aggregation over the same expert library. All summary tables, per-seed results, and the frozen protocol are included in the repository (\path{artifacts/cwfm/revision/}).

\subsection{Qualitative Case Studies}\label{sec:assessment-qualitative}

Aggregate metrics say how often a method succeeds; they do not show \emph{what success and failure look like} on an individual dataset. This subsection therefore complements the quantitative results with worked examples produced by a companion package in our repository, drawn from the persisted datasets of the project's classical benchmark suite. Selection is deterministic: each success example uses the seed whose primary metric is closest to the median over runs, the generator truth is revealed only afterwards for annotation, and one case is a deliberately included failure. Two remarks frame the reading. First, these cases run the classical benchmark pipelines, so they characterize the problem landscape rather than CWFM itself. Second, three cases concern temporal and latent-variable structure, outside CWFM's implemented scope; they specify, at the level of concrete mechanisms, what the planned extensions must achieve.

\subsubsection{Where Latent-Variable Errors Originate}
The final case, also outside the implemented scope, dissects error propagation in a latent-variable graph problem where the true structure is a chain over six latent variables. Figure~\ref{fig:qualgraph} compares three reconstruction levels: Level A uses the true latent scores, Level B reconstructs them from noisy indicators with cross-loadings, and Level C additionally estimates the grouping of indicators. Level A recovers the chain exactly; Levels B and C recover every true edge but add the same two spurious edges, $Z_1$-$Z_5$ and $Z_2$-$Z_6$. Because the three levels share everything except the measurement steps, the figure localizes the error: it originates in score reconstruction under cross-loadings, not in graph estimation. These cases justify the design decision that latent grouping should be propagated softly rather than fixed by a hard upstream decision.

\subsubsection{What the Case Studies Establish}
For the implemented scope, the regime and misspecification cases corroborate the RQ3 findings at the level of individual mechanisms: real regimes are recoverable with quantitatively faithful coefficient changes, and false regimes arise from misspecification with no predictive warning sign. For the planned extensions, the cases turn abstract requirements into concrete specifications: a temporal component must classify change types under calibrated error control, and a latent-variable component must carry grouping uncertainty forward.

\section{Tool Support}\label{sec:tools}

The repository accompanying this paper contains, besides the training and evaluation code used in Section~\ref{sec:assessment}, a public application layer that lets readers run the released model on concrete cases: numbered example scripts in the \texttt{examples/} folder and an interactive Streamlit web application in the \texttt{app/} folder. Both are thin clients of the same public programming interface, \texttt{cwfm.application}, which enforces the contract logic of Section~\ref{sec:approach}: every request passes through the identification gate and through the \emph{empirical} support diagnostics computed from the submitted data, returns the same typed statuses, and reports only calibrated intervals. Because the application evaluates the empirical support statistic rather than a declared support class (Section~\ref{sec:approach-gates}), its refusal rates on new data may differ from those reported in Section~\ref{sec:assessment}. The tools are also \emph{truth-safe} by construction: ordinary analysis reads only observed data and declared metadata, and the simulator ground truth stored in the repository (\texttt{truth.npz}) is never opened.

Dependencies are installed from the repository root with \texttt{python -m pip install -r requirements.txt}. The shipped model weights (\path{artifacts/cwfm/release/model_state.pt}) are verified against a recorded SHA-256 hash before any inference; if they are absent or fail the check, analyses that would need the model return an explicit \texttt{MODEL\_UNAVAILABLE} status instead of degrading silently. Each example script covers one query class or one aspect of the system's behavior (Table~\ref{tab:examples}) and accepts common options for seeding, device selection, output redirection, machine-readable output, and a dry-run mode that validates the inputs and the declared contract without running inference.

The fourth script exercises the property this paper emphasizes: it submits a sequence of contrasts of which some are answerable and some are not (an observational effect without declared identification, a network contrast with inadequate exposure support), and shows that the system answers the former and refuses the latter with a machine-readable reason, exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:assessment}.

The browser interface, started with \texttt{streamlit run app/streamlit\_app.py}, offers four working views. \emph{Analyze} runs a single query of any of the three implemented classes on a selected repository case and returns either an estimate with its calibrated interval or a typed refusal with its reason. \emph{Compare} repeats one analysis across comparable cases and juxtaposes estimates, interval widths, and abstention decisions. \emph{Benchmark Atlas} browses the stored results of the classical reference evaluators without model inference. \emph{Model Diagnostics} exposes, for one analysis, the weights the router assigns to each classical estimator, the compiled versus final estimate, and the state of the neural components. Because the application calls the same public interface as the scripts, no view can bypass a gate: the tools are not a demonstration layered on top of the system but the system itself, exercised through its only public entry point.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Results Mean}\label{sec:discussion-meaning}

The results support a specific reading of what a pretrained system for causal inference should be. The measured performance did not come from a neural network that estimates effects better than classical statistics: it came from the compiled classical pathway, a library of classical estimators applied correctly, blended by a pretrained router, behind deterministic contract logic. The foundation-model machinery earned its place by \emph{selecting and guarding} classical inference, not by replacing it.

The confirmatory round sharpens this reading. With tuning, calibration, and test data disjoint by construction, a shallow random-forest risk selector and a convex stack both exceed the frozen deep router by small but statistically resolvable margins, and a one-parameter temperature rescaling improves the router's own logits (Table~\ref{tab:confirmatory}). At the evaluated checkpoint, the pretrained transformer is therefore not yet the best available implementation of its own routing role. What the experiments do establish is the value of the surrounding envelope of typed contracts, deterministic gates, one uniform conformal protocol, and refusal as a first-class outcome, which the property test shows wraps any of these selectors identically. This locates where the returns currently are: reliability engineering contributes demonstrated value, while deep amortization of estimator selection is a precisely priced opportunity for improvement.

We believe this is the realistic near-term value of the approach. Matching a correctly chosen specialist is of limited interest when the specialist is known; the practical problem is that in real analyses nobody tells the analyst which specialist is correct, and Table~\ref{tab:scenario} shows how costly a wrong choice is. A single system that is close to the winner in most cells, refuses the queries whose declared conditions fail, and reports calibrated intervals removes exactly the decisions that make applied causal analysis fragile. The zero false-answer rate was achieved by deterministic enforcement of a declared contract and verified by experiment, an ordering we consider healthier than hoping that a learned system happens to behave safely.

The safety-first selection protocol also deserves comment. The evaluated checkpoint was not the most accurate candidate seen during training; candidates with better average scores were discarded because they violated a safety constraint. The cost is equally visible: the surviving checkpoint predates the residual-training stage, so the same constraint that protected structural specificity also left the free-form neural components untrained (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5}). A training protocol that preserves the constraint while letting the later stages mature, for example by freezing the calibrated regime decision once it reaches the desired specificity, is the obvious next design iteration. Encoding such trades in the selection procedure rather than in the narrative is, in our view, a practice worth adopting for learned causal systems in general.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

The assessment was designed to expose the study's limitations, and we summarize them here rather than restating the full analyses.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Untrained neural components.} Safety-constrained selection favored a checkpoint from before the residual-training stage, so the gated residual and the world-particle objective are untested rather than merely unhelpful; whether the no-harm residual can deliver safe improvements remains an open question (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-rq5}).
\item \emph{Routing accuracy.} The router keeps all experts active instead of committing to the best one, leaving measurable headroom in the threshold and null cells, and the confirmatory round shows that shallow non-neural aggregators over the same library match or slightly exceed it. An accuracy contribution of deep pretraining to estimator routing is therefore not demonstrated at this checkpoint.
\item \emph{Presentation invariance.} At the locked evaluation, reordering columns shifted the estimate by roughly the typical estimation error. The two root causes were repaired in the estimator layer and the repair was verified on the frozen invariance bank (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory}); measurable sensitivity remains only under probes that genuinely change the data.
\item \emph{Generalization evidence.} The RQ4 results are development diagnostics resting on four episodes per family, and accuracy collapses on the one family qualitatively unlike the training prior without the system recognizing the mismatch.
\item \emph{Scope.} The implementation covers static tabular effects, observed regimes, and network interference; temporal changes, latent variables, and partially specified graphs are design goals, not evaluated capabilities.
\item \emph{Statistical resolution.} The locked bank has 30 episodes per cell, so a single episode moves any proportion by 3.3 percentage points. The confirmatory stream raises the effect-estimation resolution to 300 episodes per scenario, but the regime cells were not re-run at that scale, and only one training run was performed.
\item \emph{Support statistic.} Both rounds exercise declared support classes; the finite-sample operating characteristics of the empirical support statistic of the public runtime remain unbenchmarked.
\item \emph{Synthetic evidence.} All evidence is synthetic or semi-synthetic: the evaluation banks come from the same kind of simulator as the training data, and the external benchmark grafts a synthetic treatment layer onto real covariates from a single domain. The reported accuracy is therefore an upper bound on what should be expected on real data, and no numerical cross-system claim is made against other causal foundation models (Section~\ref{sec:assessment-comparison}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What a Causal Foundation Model Cannot Solve}\label{sec:discussion-cannot}

Some limits are not engineering debts but mathematical facts, and no amount of pretraining removes them. Hidden confounding cannot be overcome from the same observational data without additional assumptions, instruments, or experiments. No model can estimate a contrast in a region where no comparable data exist. And when several causal structures explain the observed data equally well, the direction of an edge inside that equivalence class is not recoverable from the data alone. A foundation model does not eliminate causal assumptions; it \emph{moves} them into the pretraining distribution. This relocation has a real benefit, because one explicit, versioned, and stress-tested prior replaces thousands of implicit per-analysis choices, but it makes the prior part of the scientific claim. It should be published, criticized, and revised like any other assumption.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:discussion-future}

The findings define a concrete agenda. On the current scope, the first priority is sharper episode-level risk prediction, for which the confirmatory round supplies both a measurable target and a training signal: the shallow selector shows that expert estimates, standard errors, and episode metadata carry enough information to improve on the frozen logits, and temperature scaling shows those logits are well ordered but under-concentrated. Distilling the shallow selector's inputs into the pretrained router, or simply deploying the temperature-scaled or stacked policy behind the same gates, are the immediate options; world particles that actually specialize are a second target, and the frozen invariance bank should run as a routine regression test. On scope, the next extensions are temporal mechanism changes, latent-variable structure, and identification with partially specified graphs, together with sensitivity bounds so that near-violations of assumptions yield widened intervals rather than refusals; the qualitative case studies of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-qualitative} specify what these extensions must achieve. On evidence, the essential step is leaving the synthetic world: evaluation on semi-synthetic benchmarks built from real covariate distributions, and on real datasets with randomized or quasi-experimental ground truth. Rich operational event data, for example from business processes and inventory systems, offer an attractive application domain, and electronics-relevant settings are equally natural first targets: networked sensors, industrial process control, fault-diagnosis interventions, and energy-management policies in distributed cyber-physical systems combine explicit intervention points with exactly the network structure the interference component models. The semi-synthetic appliances-energy benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:assessment-confirmatory} is a first step of this kind; datasets with real, rather than grafted, intervention layers are the necessary continuation. Still outstanding on methodology are multiple independent training seeds, an out-of-distribution family bank never used in checkpoint selection, a dedicated evaluation of the empirical support statistic, and a confirmatory-scale re-run of the regime-determination cells.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced CWFM, a system that treats reliability in causal inference as a design requirement. Given a dataset and a formally specified causal question, CWFM fits and combines classical estimators through a pretrained risk router, checks a declared contract of identification and support conditions with deterministic gates before producing any estimate, reports only conformally calibrated uncertainty, and refuses, with a stated reason, the queries whose conditions fail. On a locked synthetic benchmark, this single pretrained checkpoint matched the pooled accuracy of the best fixed baseline without knowing which mechanism generated each dataset, kept its intervals near nominal episode-level coverage, and answered none of the 120 queries whose declared conditions failed, while every baseline answered all of them. Every reported estimate is produced by the compiled classical estimator blend; the free-form neural components remained untrained at the selected checkpoint, their contribution an open opportunity rather than a demonstrated fact. A protocol-frozen confirmatory round on fresh episode streams reproduced these results without touching a single neural parameter, verified the repair of the presentation-invariance defect, extended the evidence to a semi-synthetic benchmark on real energy-sensor covariates, and delivered the study's most instructive finding: shallow non-neural aggregators over the same expert library match or slightly exceed the frozen router. The demonstrated contribution of this work is therefore the reliability envelope of typed queries, deterministic refusal, and calibrated uncertainty around orchestrated classical estimators, while a genuine accuracy advantage of deep pretrained routing remains to be shown.

We close with the test that we believe should define success for this line of research. A causal model that detects an effect under hidden confounding in every run, while its estimate is badly biased and its intervals never contain the truth, has not advanced causal inference; it has automated false confidence. The measure of progress is therefore not only how accurately a model answers, but whether it knows what it cannot know: whether it separates identified from unidentified questions, supported from unsupported contrasts, and calibrated confidence from mere fluency. CWFM shows that this behavior can be obtained without giving up pooled accuracy on the questions that can be answered; recognizing the failures the contract does not declare, above all data from outside the training prior, is the clearly identified next step. We hope this standard, answer well and refuse well, becomes the default acceptance test for systems that claim to reason about cause and effect.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Many of the most important questions in science, medicine, and industry are \emph{causal}: What happens to the outcome if we change the treatment, the price, the policy, or the process? Standard machine-learning models are built to answer a different question, namely what is likely to happen if nothing is changed. A model can predict outcomes very accurately and still be completely wrong about the consequences of an \emph{intervention}, because prediction exploits every correlation in the data, including correlations that would disappear the moment we intervene~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. Answering causal questions therefore requires dedicated methods, and those methods rest on \emph{assumptions} about how the data were generated.

This is where causal analysis becomes fragile in practice. Before seeing the answer, the analyst must choose a model class, an estimator, an adjustment strategy, and often more specialized ingredients such as how influence spreads through a network. Each choice is an assumption, and a wrong choice does not merely reduce accuracy; it can produce conclusions that are \emph{confidently wrong}. Two examples from our own benchmarks illustrate this. First, when a linear model is fitted to data generated by a single smooth nonlinear mechanism, it can ``discover'' a change in the causal mechanism that does not exist, while its test loss improves substantially; the better fit rewards the wrong structural conclusion. Second, when an unobserved common cause affects both treatment and outcome, a standard estimator can detect an effect in every run while its estimate is severely biased and its confidence interval never contains the truth. In both cases the analysis looks successful by the usual metrics and is wrong precisely where it matters.

The state of the art offers two partial answers, each with a gap. On one side, classical causal inference provides a rich theory of \emph{identification} (determining whether a causal question can be answered at all from the available data and assumptions), together with well-understood estimators~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif,hudgens2008interference}. But every new dataset requires the analyst to select and configure these tools by hand, and the examples above show how easily that selection goes wrong. On the other side, a recent line of work pretrains transformer models on large collections of synthetic causal problems, so that a new dataset can be analyzed in a single forward pass without refitting~\cite{ma2025causalfm,robertson2025dopfn,thompson2026arrow,qiao2026cdfm}. These \emph{causal foundation models} remove the manual selection burden, but they inherit a different risk: they \emph{always return an answer}, even when the question is not answerable from the data, thereby automating exactly the false confidence described above.

In this paper we propose the \textbf{Causal World Foundation Model (CWFM)}, a foundation model designed to combine the strengths of both sides. CWFM is a transformer pretrained on a large stream of synthetic causal problems. Given a new dataset together with a formally specified causal question, it answers \emph{in context}, without retraining. Two design choices distinguish it from prior causal foundation models. First, instead of acting as one universal neural estimator, CWFM learns to \emph{select and combine} a library of well-understood classical estimators, adding a learned correction only when it demonstrably \emph{does no harm}. Second, the model is wrapped in an explicit \emph{reliability layer}: a deterministic identification check and a data-support check decide, before any estimate is produced, whether the question can be answered at all. When it cannot, CWFM \emph{refuses} and explains why, instead of returning a misleading number. Its uncertainty intervals are \emph{calibrated} with finite-sample conformal methods rather than taken directly from the network.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
\item the CWFM architecture, which integrates a pretrained causal encoder, a risk-routed library of classical estimators, formal identification and support gates, and \emph{calibrated abstention} in a single query-conditioned model;
\item a training and model-selection protocol in which safety requirements, such as never harming the classical estimate and never answering unanswerable queries, are enforced as \emph{hard constraints} rather than soft objectives;
\item a reproducible evaluation protocol with strictly separated training, development, calibration, and locked test streams, covering both answerable queries and stress queries that should be refused;
\item an open reference implementation of the model, the synthetic problem generators, and the complete experimental pipeline.
\end{itemize}

Our assessment is organized around five research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] \emph{Accuracy.} Can a single pretrained model match well-tuned task-specific estimators on causal questions that are answerable from the data?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] \emph{Safety.} Does the model avoid false causal claims when a question is not identifiable or not supported by the data?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] \emph{Structure.} Can the model detect genuine changes in a causal mechanism while rejecting spurious changes induced by model misspecification?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] \emph{Generalization.} How does the model behave on problem families that were held out from its pretraining distribution?
\item[\textbf{RQ5}] \emph{Attribution.} Which components of the architecture are actually responsible for the observed performance?
\end{itemize}

The experimental results can be summarized as follows. On answerable questions, CWFM matches the accuracy of the best task-specific estimator in each setting, without knowing in advance which setting it is in, and clearly outperforms a generic flexible baseline, with uncertainty intervals close to their nominal coverage (RQ1). It refuses every stress query that should be refused, while baseline estimators return a number in all of them (RQ2). It recovers genuine mechanism changes far more often than the previous version of the system and, unlike a standard model-based splitting baseline, largely resists the temptation to split a smooth nonlinear mechanism into fake regimes (RQ3). Generalization is good under moderate distribution shift but degrades sharply on one problem family that is qualitatively absent from pretraining, which we report as an honest limitation (RQ4). Finally, the gains are attributable to the estimator library, the risk-based routing, and the deterministic safety logic; the free-form neural components contribute little yet, which we also report openly (RQ5).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on causal inference and causal foundation models. Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} introduces the necessary background concepts. Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents and motivates the CWFM architecture. Section~\ref{sec:assessment} describes the experimental design and answers the five research questions. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Causal Inference and Causal Discovery}\label{sec:related-causal}

Structural causal models and the potential-outcomes framework give causal questions a precise meaning~\cite{pearl2009causality,hernan2020whatif}. They distinguish three tasks that look similar but are fundamentally different: predicting an outcome under the observed regime, estimating the effect of an intervention, and reasoning about what would have happened in a particular case under a different action. Their central tool is the theory of \emph{identification}: a set of rules, such as back-door adjustment, that determine whether a causal question can be answered from the available data under stated assumptions. When point identification fails, the theory does not stop; sensitivity analysis quantifies how conclusions change as hidden confounding grows~\cite{rosenbaum1983sensitivity}, and partial identification replaces a point estimate with an honest interval~\cite{manski1990bounds}. The framework also covers settings where units influence one another, such as treatments that spill over through a network~\cite{hudgens2008interference}.

A complementary line of research, \emph{causal discovery}, aims to learn the causal structure itself from data. Approaches include constraint-based algorithms~\cite{spirtes2000causation}, continuous optimization over graph structures~\cite{zheng2018notears}, invariance of causal mechanisms across environments~\cite{peters2016invariant}, discovery from heterogeneous and nonstationary data~\cite{huang2020heterogeneous}, and causal analysis of nonlinear time series~\cite{runge2019pcmci}. All of these methods are powerful, but they share a practical burden: for every new dataset, an expert must choose the algorithm, the model class, and the tuning parameters, and the true structure is not available to check the choice. This manual selection step is exactly where the failures described in the introduction arise, and it is the entry point for foundation models.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Causal Tasks}\label{sec:related-cfm}

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) offer a way to remove the manual selection step. A transformer is pretrained on a large collection of synthetic problems whose ground truth is known by construction; afterwards, a new dataset is processed in a single forward pass, and the network approximates Bayesian inference under the distribution of problems it was trained on~\cite{muller2022pfns}. This idea has produced strong general-purpose tabular models~\cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} and has recently been carried over to causal questions. For causal-effect estimation, CausalFM builds PFNs from priors over structural causal models covering back-door, front-door, and instrumental-variable settings~\cite{ma2025causalfm}; Do-PFN predicts interventional outcome distributions directly from observational data~\cite{robertson2025dopfn}; and CausalPFN amortizes average and heterogeneous treatment-effect estimation~\cite{balazadeh2025causalpfn}. For causal discovery, Arrow predicts a graph skeleton together with a variable ordering, which guarantees an acyclic result~\cite{thompson2026arrow}; TabCausal and CDFM predict causal edges across heterogeneous tabular problems~\cite{li2026tabcausal,qiao2026cdfm}; and partial background knowledge can be supplied to such models instead of being ignored~\cite{reuter2026partialgraphs}. Extensions address temporal data with interventions~\cite{thumm2026causaltimeprior}, longitudinal treatment sequences~\cite{zare2026causallongpfn}, and amortized sensitivity analysis~\cite{javurek2026sensitivitypfn}.

These models establish that causal inference can be amortized, but two limitations recur. First, the synthetic pretraining distribution is itself a causal assumption: pretraining cannot make an unidentifiable question identifiable, and a dataset generated by a mechanism outside the prior can be answered with unwarranted confidence. Second, and most importantly for this paper, these models \emph{always return an answer}. None of them combines amortized estimation with a formal check of whether the submitted question is answerable at all, a check of whether the requested contrast is supported by the data, and the option to refuse.

\subsection{Reliability, Calibration, and Abstention}\label{sec:related-reliability}

The ingredients for such checks exist, but largely outside the foundation-model literature. Identifiability is a property of the causal model and its assumptions, not a pattern that can be certified from the data alone; learned ``identifiability scores'' are therefore heuristics that reflect the training prior, and even amortized sensitivity analysis can only learn the bounds of a partially identified effect, never its exact position inside them~\cite{ma2025causalfm,javurek2026sensitivitypfn}. When no ground truth is available, self-compatibility checks can falsify a causal analysis by testing whether structures learned from different subsets of variables agree~\cite{faller2024selfcompatibility}. On the uncertainty side, conformal prediction turns any point predictor into intervals with finite-sample validity guarantees~\cite{vovk2005conformal,lei2018distribution}, and selective prediction studies the trade-off between answering fewer questions and answering them more reliably~\cite{geifman2017selective}. Each of these tools is well understood in isolation; what is missing is their integration into an amortized causal model, so that identification, support, calibration, and refusal are produced together with the estimate rather than left to the analyst.

\subsection{Positioning and Research Gap}\label{sec:related-gap}

In summary, two research traditions approach causal questions from opposite directions. Classical causal inference is identification-aware and statistically rigorous, but every analysis must be assembled by hand, and the required choices are error-prone. Causal foundation models remove the manual effort through pretraining, but they answer unconditionally and provide no built-in notion of when an answer is unjustified. CWFM is positioned at the intersection of the two: it uses a pretrained model to amortize the choice and combination of estimators, while a deterministic identification layer, a query-specific support check, conformal calibration, and an explicit abstention decision guarantee that the convenience of a foundation model does not come at the price of false causal confidence. To our knowledge, no existing causal foundation model treats refusal as a first-class output and enforces it as a hard constraint during model selection.

Manuscript: Estimating Causal Effects from Tabular Data with Assumption Gates and Automated Estimator Selection for Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence
Revision date: 8 September 2026

We thank the reviewers for their detailed comments. The revised manuscript presents the framework as an inspectable integration of causal assumptions, classical estimation, automated aggregation, and documented decisions. Its main empirical finding is constructive: simple aggregation improves accuracy within this shared framework. The shallow risk selector and convex stack reduce pooled mean absolute error by 12.4% and 8.8%, respectively, relative to the evaluated deep router on 1,800 confirmatory queries.

We clarified the conditions supporting these findings, corrected two support-checking defects and one assumption-entry issue in the public application, and added a focused empirical support audit. We also expanded the background and method explanations where readers would benefit from a slower introduction: identification versus estimation, a treatment-query walkthrough, routing weights, calibration, and the interpretation of evaluation metrics. The corrected Reviewer 1 comments are addressed with a matched pretraining ablation, router diagnostics, individual development results, explicit acceptance denominators, and a computational-cost audit.

REVIEWER 1

Comment 1. Compare the model with and without pretraining and explain its contribution.

Response. We added a matched ablation of the evaluated effect-estimation pathway. The released pretrained checkpoint is compared with five untrained copies of exactly the same architecture, using initialization seeds 1729 to 1733. All models receive the same observed inputs, fitted classical candidates, compatibility masks, and declared gate. The untrained copies receive no optimization or adaptation, and simulator targets are excluded from model inputs. Residual contributions are below 2.1e-7 in all models, so the comparison isolates pretrained encoder and router weights to numerical precision. Classical estimators continue to be fitted to each dataset in both conditions.

On the existing 1,800-episode confirmatory effect bank, pooled mean absolute error (MAE) is 0.1612 with pretraining and 0.1673 averaged across the five untrained initializations, a 3.7% reduction. Individual untrained MAEs range from 0.1654 to 0.1711. The paired reduction is 0.0061, with a 95% bootstrap interval of [0.0047, 0.0076]. Uniform aggregation gives 0.1683. Pretraining reduces the scenario mean in five of six cells; threshold interference favors the untrained and uniform controls. Table 11 reports every scenario, and the released CSV contains every initialization and episode.

This provides direct evidence that pretraining learns useful candidate preferences beyond random initialization and equal weighting. It does not establish superiority over the shallow selector or convex stack, which retain the stronger main results. We identify the ablation as a post-review analysis of an existing bank, not a newly independent confirmatory study. The intervals condition on one trained checkpoint and five fixed initializations. This is an effect-routing ablation, not a repeated-training study or an ablation of the classical regime decision rule. The protocol was specified before the comparison; it changes no checkpoint, calibration, or support threshold.

Locations: Sections 5.8, 6.10, and 7.1; Table 11. Reproduction: python -m cwfm.pretraining_experiment; experiments/pretraining_protocol.json; artifacts/cwfm/pretraining_revision/.

Comment 4. Explain and analyze what the router has learned.

Response. We expanded the explanation and added a control that separates average candidate preferences from episode-specific adaptation. During pretraining, simulator effect truth provides a soft target that gives greater mass to candidates with smaller absolute error. The loss also rewards the accuracy of the combined estimate and discourages concentration on too few candidates. At inference, the encoder summarizes the observed dataset and query; the router also receives candidate estimates and standard errors. Its masked scores become nonnegative weights summing to one. No simulator truth enters this calculation.

The new analysis reports candidate weights, normalized entropy, within-group variation, and oracle top-choice diagnostics for every evaluated episode. The trained weights remain broad rather than selecting a single estimator: scenario-average normalized entropy ranges from 0.977 to 0.987, where one is uniform. Static linear and nonlinear episodes give interaction candidates mean weights of 0.238 and 0.236, compared with 0.110 and 0.111 for augmented inverse probability weighting. Network cells give interaction candidates approximately 0.230 to 0.234 and spline candidates 0.114 to 0.126. These weights describe contributions to an estimate, not probabilities that a causal model is true.

We also replace each episode's weights with the pretrained weights averaged over the separate tuning stream, matching task and compatibility mask. This fixed-mean control has pooled MAE 0.1621, compared with 0.1612 for episode-specific routing. The paired improvement is 0.0009, with a 95% interval of [0.0003, 0.0015]. Together with the pretraining ablation, this shows that the evaluated checkpoint mainly learns useful broad aggregation preferences, with a smaller additional benefit from adapting weights to individual datasets. We report that finding constructively and retain the stronger shallow-policy results.

Comment 3. Benchmark the finite-sample empirical support statistic used by the application.

Response. We added a direct audit of the actual public screening functions, including the minimum treatment-group count condition. It uses sample sizes 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512; two static assignment families with six severities each; and a degree-four network with four assignment probabilities. Every cell has 500 independent dataset replicates. There is no effect fitting, neural inference, retraining, or threshold tuning.

The reference is the existing practical-overlap policy applied to a known population distribution. It is not strict mathematical positivity or causal correctness. We define false refusal as rejection in a population-adequate cell and false acceptance as acceptance in a population-inadequate cell. If a positive decision means detecting inadequate support, the false-positive rate is the false-refusal rate. Both error directions are reported, with pointwise 95% Wilson intervals.

For linear assignment at slope one, false refusals decrease from 0.528 at n=32 to 0.106 at n=128 and zero at n=512. For quadratic assignment at slope two, all 500 datasets are accepted at both n=128 and n=512 despite failing the population reference. Increasing sample size does not repair a misspecified linear-probability screen. In the network setting at assignment probability 0.3, false refusal remains 0.246 at n=512. The audit also records consistent refusal at linear-assignment slopes four and eight and network probabilities 0.1 and 0.02. It identifies both useful screening behavior and specific settings requiring a more flexible diagnostic.

The audit also motivated two small correctness repairs: a passing exposure check can no longer overwrite an earlier treatment-group support failure, and a missing own-treatment group cannot pass the exposure function. The numerical thresholds are unchanged. The audit evaluates this corrected composition, and regression tests cover both defects. The revised method separates treatment-score, exposure-quantile, and group-count requirements into readable paragraphs. Discussion explains why additional observations help several settings while a linear score cannot capture symmetric quadratic assignment.

Locations: Sections 4.5, 5.7, 6.8, 7.1, and 7.2; Figure 12; Table 10. Reproduction: experiments/support_protocol.json, cwfm/support_experiment.py, and artifacts/cwfm/support_revision/. The artifacts include all per-replicate decisions, all 80 summaries, intervals, code and data hashes, and environment information.
